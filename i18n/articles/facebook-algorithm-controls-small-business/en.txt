== body ==
<p>If you're asking how to stay ahead of social media algorithm changes, here is the honest answer: you can't out-guess them, but you can make your posts easy to match. Feeds keep getting better at learning what each person wants, and as of this week people can simply tell Facebook. Your job is to make it obvious which interest every post serves.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-adds-plain-language-algorithm-controls/832910/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Social Media Today</a> reported on October 13 that Facebook now lets users describe, in everyday language, what they want to see more or less of in Feed and Reels. Facebook calls it "an algorithm you can actually talk to" and says the feed adapts within the same session. After someone taps "Interested" or "Not interested," Facebook may ask a follow-up question about what they liked or disliked.</p>

<h2 id="what-exactly-did-facebook-change">What exactly did Facebook change?</h2>
<p>The update builds on Facebook's existing "Your Algorithm" control, where people can add or remove topics the system has guessed for them. Now there is also a chatbot-style prompt where they can type what they like in their own words. Facebook says the feature runs on large language models (LLMs), which it says "can incorporate the interests, context, and intent behind natural language."</p>
<p>Two details matter for a small business. First, changes apply right away, not next week. Second, the "Not interested" button now opens a short conversation, which can tell the system more precisely what someone doesn't want.</p>
<p>Social Media Today also points out that most people rarely touch their settings. So don't expect your whole audience to start chatting with the feed. Expect your most engaged followers, the ones whose reactions carry the most weight, to be the first to try it.</p>

<h2 id="does-this-hurt-organic-reach-for-business-pages">Does this hurt organic reach for business pages?</h2>
<p>Nobody outside Meta knows yet, and anyone quoting you a percentage is guessing. What we can say is where the pressure points are.</p>
<p>Instagram head Adam Mosseri explained the logic of ranking in a set of videos covered by <a href="https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-chief-shares-algorithm-insights-and-posting-tips/831326/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Social Media Today</a> in September. Without ranking, he said, feeds would be flooded by the accounts that post most, "and business and brands post a lot more than creators and people." In other words, ranking exists partly to hold brand volume back.</p>
<p>He also said ranking is built on predictions of how people will respond to a post, such as whether they'll watch it or share it with a friend. Original content gets priority over reposts, and newer posts get more exposure, which is why the feed is "roughly in chronological order."</p>
<p>Put that next to Facebook's update and the direction is clear. A business post earns a slot when it predicts a real reaction from someone who wants that topic. Posting more of the same doesn't buy you a slot.</p>

<h2 id="why-does-chasing-algorithm-rumors-keep-failing">Why does chasing algorithm rumors keep failing?</h2>
<p>Economists call it herd behavior: when people are unsure, they copy what everyone else seems to be doing, assuming the crowd knows something they don't. On social media it looks like every bakery using the same trending audio and every consultant posting the same carousel.</p>
<p>The trouble is that when everyone copies, nobody stands out. ToucHigh's competitive-content research found that about 87% of social content crowds into the same "authentic" formula. Even authenticity has become a template.</p>
<p>That's the trap. The trend you borrowed may not fit your audience, so the people who see it don't react, and the system learns your posts aren't for them. Now that users can say "less of this" in plain words, an off-brand trend can teach the feed to show you to fewer of the right people.</p>

<h2 id="what-should-a-small-business-change-this-week">What should a small business change this week?</h2>
<p>Here is a one-hour routine you can run on Monday morning.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Write your topics in customer words.</strong> List the two or three things your customers would type if Facebook asked what they want to see. "Easy weeknight dinners," not "culinary solutions." "Furniture for small apartments," not "lifestyle inspiration."</li>
<li><strong>Tag your last 20 posts.</strong> Mark each one with the topic it serves. Anything that fits none of them was probably a borrowed trend. Compare how those posts did against the rest.</li>
<li><strong>Name the topic early.</strong> Say what the post is about in the first line of the caption and in the first seconds of a video. Facebook hasn't said how it matches posts to stated interests, but a system built on language models reads language, so plain words are a sensible bet.</li>
<li><strong>Favor originals.</strong> Film your own product, your own workshop, your own answers to customer questions. Mosseri said original content from the original creator gets priority over reposts.</li>
<li><strong>Test one new idea at a time.</strong> On Instagram, Mosseri suggested trial Reels for trying new approaches. Run one experiment a week instead of rebuilding your plan every time a rumor spreads.</li>
<li><strong>Track reactions, not reach.</strong> Every Friday, check shares, saves and comments per post. Mosseri advised focusing on engagement rate rather than total views or likes.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="how-do-you-stay-visible-without-sounding-like-everyone-else">How do you stay visible without sounding like everyone else?</h2>
<p>The real answer to "how do I stay ahead of algorithm changes" is boring and reliable: be specific. Specific customers, specific problems, specific proof. A furniture maker who posts "how we fit a desk into a 6-foot alcove" gives the feed a clear topic and gives viewers a reason to send it to a friend with the same awkward alcove.</p>
<p>A few habits help:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use your real voice, including the phrases customers use in your DMs and reviews.</li>
<li>Show work in progress, mistakes and fixes. Those are hard to copy and easy to recognize.</li>
<li>Answer the questions customers already ask. Each answer matches an interest someone could state out loud.</li>
<li>Keep a "not for us" list of trends you'll skip, so you don't relitigate it every week.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="what-to-watch-over-the-next-month">What to watch over the next month</h2>
<p>Two signals are worth tracking. First, compare reach on posts that fit your core topics with posts that don't. If the gap widens, the new controls are working against borrowed content. Second, read your comments for the words people use to describe what they like about you. Those are probably close to the words they'd use when they talk to the feed, so use them in your captions.</p>

== faq ==
Q: Can I see what people told Facebook's algorithm about my page?
A: Not as far as anyone knows. The plain-language controls described so far shape each user's own feed, and Facebook hasn't said it will share those requests with Pages. What you can see is how people respond to your posts. Compare shares, comments and saves on posts about your core topics with off-topic posts to see which way the new controls are pushing.
Q: Should I switch my Facebook strategy to Reels only?
A: Not automatically. Facebook's new controls work in both Feed and Reels, so a clear topic matters in either format. Video does make it easy to show real products and real people, which helps you look original. Test a few Reels on your core topics, compare their shares and comments with your photo and text posts, and shift your mix based on what your own audience does.
Q: How often should a small business post on Facebook and Instagram now?
A: Meta hasn't published a magic number. Instagram's Adam Mosseri said to post as often as you can to raise your odds of a hit, while avoiding burnout. For most small teams that means a steady pace you can keep up with real quality, then a gradual increase once you have a backlog of ideas. A consistent rhythm you can sustain beats a burst followed by silence.
Q: What is herd behavior in social media marketing?
A: Herd behavior is the habit of copying what others do when you're unsure, on the assumption that the crowd knows best. On social media it shows up as every brand using the same trend, format or caption style. It feels safe, but it makes brands look interchangeable, and it can attract viewers who were never going to become customers.
