<p>Which social media metrics actually show whether a small business has influence? The social media metrics that matter are the ones that show people acting on what you post: sharing it, saving it, replying in real sentences, clicking through and buying. Follower count tells you how many people once said yes. It doesn't tell you whether anyone is listening now.</p>
<p>There's also a new reason to rethink your scorecard. <a href="https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/ai-favors-brands-with-an-active-social-media-presence/832450/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Social Media Today</a> covered an SE Ranking study of 100,000 keywords that found social platform links in 67.66% of Google AI Overview responses and in 48.58% of non-branded Google AI Mode queries, compared with 8.4% of ChatGPT answers. Your posts aren't only reaching followers anymore. They're also raw material for AI search.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-follower-count-a-weak-metric">Why is follower count a weak metric?</h2>
<p>Three reasons. First, it's cumulative: a follower from a 2021 giveaway counts the same as a customer who bought last week. Second, it hides inactivity: feeds are ranked, so following you doesn't mean seeing you. Third, it doesn't connect to money. You can't pay rent with followers.</p>
<p>That doesn't make followers useless. A growing audience of the right people is a good thing. The problem is using followers as the main score, because it rewards tactics like giveaways and follow-for-follow that inflate the number without building a business.</p>

<h2 id="how-do-i-calculate-engagement-rate-the-useful-way">How do I calculate engagement rate the useful way?</h2>
<p>Add up the interactions on a post (reactions, comments, shares, saves), divide by the number of people it reached, and multiply by 100. A post with 40 interactions that reached 1,000 people has a 4% engagement rate.</p>
<p>Use reach, not followers, as the bottom number. If you divide by followers, a post the feed showed to only a few people looks like a flop even if those few loved it. Reach-based rates let you compare posts fairly and see which topics really land.</p>
<p>Then compare against yourself, not industry averages. Your median over the last 20 posts is your baseline. A post at double your median is a winner worth studying. One at half is a lesson.</p>

<h2 id="why-do-we-keep-measuring-the-wrong-things">Why do we keep measuring the wrong things?</h2>
<p>Performance measurement theory carries a simple warning: people work to improve whatever gets measured, so a poor measure produces poor effort. If the weekly report leads with followers, the team will chase followers, and the content will drift toward giveaways and trends that pump the number.</p>
<p>Good measures sit close to the outcome you want and are hard to fake. A save is hard to fake: someone wanted to come back. A question in your DMs is hard to fake: someone wants to buy or learn. That's what "measure the heart, not just the numbers" means in practice. Count the signals that show people care.</p>
<p>ToucHigh's competitive-content research points the same way. In one category it studied, content aimed at helping small businesses grow made up only about 10% of what rivals published, yet it drew about 35% of the engagement. Volume and impact are different scores.</p>

<h2 id="how-do-i-set-up-a-weekly-social-scorecard">How do I set up a weekly social scorecard?</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Pick one business goal per quarter.</strong> More booked appointments, more online orders or more B2B leads. Your metrics follow from it.</li>
<li><strong>Choose five to seven metrics from the table.</strong> A local salon might track saves, DMs, profile visits, booking-link clicks and bookings from social. An online store might track shares, link clicks and sales from tagged links.</li>
<li><strong>Tag every link.</strong> Add UTM tags (short labels at the end of a URL that tell your analytics where a visit came from) to every link in your bio, posts and stories.</li>
<li><strong>Set your baseline.</strong> Pull your last 20 posts, calculate each metric and write down the median.</li>
<li><strong>Review every Friday for 15 minutes.</strong> Note the top and bottom post by engagement rate, and write one sentence on why you think each landed where it did.</li>
<li><strong>Check AI citations monthly.</strong> Ask Google AI Mode, ChatGPT and Perplexity five questions your customers ask, and note whether your brand, site or posts appear in the answers or sources.</li>
<li><strong>Change one thing at a time.</strong> Adjust a topic, a format or a posting time, not all three, so you know what moved the number.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="can-you-predict-which-posts-will-perform-before-you-publish">Can you predict which posts will perform before you publish?</h2>
<p>Partly. Predicted-engagement scoring compares a draft with patterns in large sets of past content and estimates how it's likely to do. It's a useful filter for picking the strongest of three drafts or catching a weak opening line before it goes live.</p>
<p>It is not a guarantee. Treat a predicted score as a pre-launch estimate and your weekly scorecard as the verdict. Over time, compare the two: if high-scoring posts keep beating your median, the score is earning its place in your process.</p>

== faq ==
Q: What is a good engagement rate for a small business?
A: It depends on the platform, the format and your audience, so industry averages can mislead. The most useful benchmark is your own median engagement rate per reach across your last 20 posts. Aim to lift that median steadily, and study any post that lands at double it. Comparing yourself with your own history shows real progress.
Q: Do followers matter at all anymore?
A: Yes, but as context, not as the main score. A growing audience of the right people gives each post a bigger starting pool. The trouble starts when follower count drives decisions, because it rewards giveaways and trends that bring in people who will never buy. Check follower growth monthly and judge content by engagement, clicks and inquiries.
Q: How do I know if AI search tools cite my social posts?
A: Check by hand once a month. Write five questions your customers ask, then ask them in Google AI Mode, regular Google search (for AI Overviews), ChatGPT and Perplexity. Look at the sources each answer lists and note whether your website, social posts or profiles appear. Keep the same questions every month so you can see the trend.
Q: Which free tools can track these metrics?
A: Each platform's built-in analytics, such as Meta Business Suite and LinkedIn page analytics, covers reach, engagement, saves and shares. Google Analytics, combined with UTM-tagged links, shows visits and sales that came from social. A simple spreadsheet works as your weekly scorecard. Add paid tools only once you know which numbers you actually use to make decisions.
