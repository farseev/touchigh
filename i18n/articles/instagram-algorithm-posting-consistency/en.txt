== body ==
<p>How often should a brand post on Instagram? Short answer: steady beats frequent. Pick a pace your team can keep up for 12 straight weeks, then split planning, production and publishing into fixed batches, so "what do we post today?" stops eating your brainpower every single day.</p>
<p>There's now clearer evidence for this. According to <a href="https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-chief-shares-algorithm-insights-and-posting-tips/831326/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Social Media Today</a>, Instagram head Adam Mosseri publicly walked through how the feed is ranked in late September. The feed is roughly chronological, and newer posts get more exposure. He also said plainly that businesses and brands post far more than creators and regular users do, and that without ranking, high-frequency business accounts would flood the feed.</p>
<p>Put those two points together and the message for brands selling overseas, including Chinese brands entering the US, is simple. Go quiet and you vanish from the timeline. Pile on volume and the algorithm won't reward you either. What gets results is a sustainable, steady rhythm, plus posts that are each worth watching to the end and sending to a friend.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-mosseri-say-and-what-does-it-mean-for-brand-account">What did Mosseri say, and what does it mean for brand accounts?</h2>
<p>Based on Social Media Today's summary, Mosseri's main points boil down to the list below, each followed by our take for brands selling abroad:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ranking runs on predictions.</strong> The system predicts whether people will watch to the end and whether they'll send a post to a friend. Brand posts compete for the same attention as posts from friends and creators, so filler starts at a disadvantage.</li>
<li><strong>Freshness carries a lot of weight.</strong> The feed is roughly chronological. Skip two weeks and your followers barely see you; showing up steadily is worth more than an occasional burst.</li>
<li><strong>Originality comes first.</strong> Original content from the original creator is prioritized over reposts. Reuploading someone else's video rarely earns priority distribution.</li>
<li><strong>Watch engagement rate, not total views.</strong> He recommends judging performance by engagement rate rather than total views or likes.</li>
<li><strong>Use trial Reels to test new ideas.</strong> Trends move fast, and what worked last week may not work this week. Trial Reels are a low-risk way to experiment.</li>
<li><strong>Don't burn yourself out.</strong> Many creators post as much as they can and hope something hits. Mosseri reminds them to manage their time and avoid burnout.</li>
</ul>
<p>Notably, Mosseri didn't give a specific posts-per-week number in the report. That means your frequency should come from your own capacity, not from an influencer's tutorial.</p>

<h2 id="why-can-t-we-stick-to-the-schedule-even-after-we-ve-set-one">Why can't we stick to the schedule even after we've set one?</h2>
<p>Most teams marketing overseas go quiet not for lack of ideas, but because every post means bouncing between seven or eight tools: Canva for graphics, CapCut for video edits, a translation tool to fix the English, a spreadsheet for the schedule, Meta Business Suite to publish, with group-chat pings and the boss's sign-off squeezed in between.</p>
<p>Educational psychology has a concept called cognitive load theory: working memory is very limited, and the more information and decisions you juggle at once, the worse each decision gets. It roughly splits load into two kinds. One is the difficulty of the task itself, such as writing natural American English copy. The other is extra load: hunting for assets, switching windows, waiting for replies, deciding on the spot what to post today.</p>
<p>It's usually the second kind that wears people down. So the fix for going quiet isn't posting less. It's cutting the extra load: make decisions in one sitting, produce in batches and let scheduling handle publishing. Once the process is simplified, the team's creative potential can finally show.</p>

<h2 id="so-how-often-should-you-post-on-instagram">So how often should you post on Instagram?</h2>
<p>There's no universal number. The safer approach is to choose a baseline frequency based on your team's capacity, run it for a full 12 weeks, then decide whether to add more. The table below gives starting points by team setup. It's rule-of-thumb advice, not a platform rule:</p>

<p>To decide whether to post more, watch one signal: after you raise frequency, does your average engagement rate drop noticeably? If it does, you're diluting quality with quantity and should step back to the previous level.</p>

<h2 id="how-do-you-run-a-plan-monthly-batch-weekly-schedule">How do you run a "plan monthly, batch weekly" schedule?</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>On the first Monday of each month, set 3–4 content pillars.</strong> For example: product use cases, common customer questions, behind the scenes at the factory and in R&amp;D, and US holidays and seasonal moments. It's mid-October, so Halloween, Thanksgiving and Black Friday should already be on your topic list.</li>
<li><strong>List the whole month's topics in one go.</strong> For each, write a one-line English hook and one line on what viewers will get from it. If you can't write those two lines, cut the topic.</li>
<li><strong>Block half a day each week for batch production.</strong> Shoot several posts in one setting, share footage between image posts and Reels, and use fixed templates so no post starts from scratch.</li>
<li><strong>Schedule posts on US time.</strong> Set times in advance in Meta Business Suite or another scheduling tool, guided by the follower-activity data in your account dashboard, so your team in China doesn't have to stay up and post by hand.</li>
<li><strong>Save 1–2 slots a week for trial Reels.</strong> Test new hooks and formats on a small scale first; if they perform, move them into your regular schedule.</li>
<li><strong>Spend 20 minutes every Friday on a review.</strong> Rank the week's posts by engagement rate. Break down why the top two worked and copy their structure; find what went wrong with the bottom one and don't repeat it next week.</li>
<li><strong>Keep 5–7 backup posts ready.</strong> Prepare evergreen content that won't go stale to cover big sales periods, business trips or sick days, so you never go dark.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="which-consistency-shortcuts-actually-hurt-your-account">Which consistency shortcuts actually hurt your account?</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Padding with reposts and reuploads.</strong> Mosseri said clearly that original content beats reposts, so borrowed content rarely gets priority distribution.</li>
<li><strong>Machine-translating Chinese drafts and copying them across platforms.</strong> The grammar may be fine, but the tone doesn't sound like an American talking. Users can tell at a glance it's a foreign seller, and engagement suffers.</li>
<li><strong>Lowering the bar to post daily.</strong> Ranking looks at whether people watch to the end and share. The more low-quality posts you publish, the worse your average engagement rate looks.</li>
<li><strong>Watching only views.</strong> One viral post can inflate views and hide an overall decline; engagement rate shows better whether your content is actually landing.</li>
</ul>

== faq ==
Q: Will a brand account get throttled for posting several times a day on Instagram?
A: There's no public information showing that posting more gets you throttled. Mosseri's explanation is that the ranking system picks content people are more likely to watch through and share, so high-frequency accounts don't flood the feed. Posting more isn't automatically punished or rewarded. If average engagement rate falls after you add posts, quality is being diluted, so step back to a lower frequency.
Q: When is the best time to post on Instagram for US followers?
A: There's no single best time. Open the insights in your Instagram professional account to see the days and hours your followers are most active, convert them to your team's local time (Beijing time for teams in China) and schedule posts. Because the feed is roughly chronological, posting when followers are active makes you easier to see. US Eastern and Pacific time are three hours apart, so test different slots if your audience is spread out.
Q: How should we use trial Reels?
A: Trial Reels are shown to non-followers first, which makes them good for testing new opening hooks, formats or topics without bothering your existing followers. Save one or two slots a week: make versions of the same product selling point with different openings, see which earns the higher engagement rate, then move the winner into your regular schedule.
Q: Can we post the same content on Instagram, Facebook and TikTok?
A: Reuse your footage, but don't copy posts as-is. Each platform has its own aspect ratio, pacing and tone, so cut and rewrite the same shoot separately for each. Originality matters too: Mosseri says original content is prioritized over reposts, and reuploading other people's content works against you in distribution.
