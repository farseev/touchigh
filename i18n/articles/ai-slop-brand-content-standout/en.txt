== body ==
<p>Using AI to create social media content quickly is entirely doable, but fast doesn't mean good. Platforms are now actively identifying and demoting generic, cookie-cutter AI content. The setup that really works isn't a more powerful writing tool. It's a new division of labor: AI handles volume and variations, your brand supplies the point of view and exclusive material, and every post gets a sameness check before it goes out.</p>
<p>LinkedIn just spelled this out. According to <a href="https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-exec-offers-insight-into-the-battle-against-ai-slop/832576/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Social Media Today</a> on October 8, LinkedIn's vice president of engineering publicly described how the platform identifies "AI slop." Its classifier now covers every post distributed beyond a member's direct connections, with 94% precision, and the "report AI slop" option launched in August was used by more than 1 million members in under three weeks.</p>
<h2 id="how-does-linkedin-identify-ai-slop">How does LinkedIn identify AI slop?</h2>
<p>According to Social Media Today, LinkedIn defines AI slop as posts that are generic and/or repetitive and don't offer real insight. Detection works in roughly three layers:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Member reports.</strong> Feedback gathered through the report option in the feed is used to train the detection system.</li>
<li><strong>A "teacher-student" setup.</strong> A larger model spots new slop patterns and generates training data, and smaller models learn from it quickly.</li>
<li><strong>Policy-based AI agents.</strong> Each agent judges posts against one specific rule, such as whether a post is overly promotional, whether it's showing off achievements, or whether it's time-sensitive. Cases the agents can't decide go to human reviewers, and the rules are updated based on their calls.</li>
</ul>
<p>LinkedIn has also previously reported that views of AI slop have dropped by 40%.</p>
<h2 id="will-ai-written-social-posts-always-get-throttled">Will AI-written social posts always get throttled?</h2>
<p>Going by LinkedIn's definition, the test is "generic, repetitive, no insight," not "was AI involved." An AI-drafted post that clearly explains a real manufacturing detail or customer problem, and a handwritten post full of empty phrases, may well meet opposite fates.</p>
<p>For cross-border sellers, "insight" isn't mysterious. A failed product sample, an analysis of why customers return items, a side-by-side test against a competitor, a tough question from an American buyer: all of these carry real information. You already have this material; it just rarely gets turned into content.</p>
<p>Other platforms may not follow LinkedIn's rules, but the logic carries over: content with nothing to say struggles to earn comments and shares anyway. For brands selling overseas, the real risk isn't being flagged as "written by AI." It's publishing something identical to what competitors post.</p>
<h2 id="why-does-everyone-s-ai-content-look-more-and-more-alike">Why does everyone's AI content look more and more alike?</h2>
<p>Behavioral economics has a familiar name for this: herd behavior. When we're unsure, we default to following the crowd. Social media is no different. When a certain hook, editing style or "authentic" caption takes off, brands rush to copy it, even if it doesn't suit their audience at all.</p>
<p>AI has dropped the cost of copying to zero. The same trending templates and the same prompts naturally produce content that looks more and more alike. ToucHigh's competitive-content research found that about 87% of social content crowds into the same "authentic" formula.</p>
<p>Flip it around, and the less crowded spots often pay better. The same research found that in the category studied, content aimed at SME growth made up only about 10% of what rivals published, yet took about 35% of engagement. The ToucHigh team's <a href="https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3805712.3808371" rel="noopener" target="_blank">paper at ACM SIGIR</a> studies exactly how agentic AI can counter this kind of content homogenization. When everyone chases trends, authenticity becomes the scarcest thing there is.</p>

<p>When choosing a tool, ask three questions. Can it use your own material and brand information? Can it reliably control tone and word choice? Can it tell you before publishing roughly how a post will perform? A tool that can't answer any of the three, however fast it is, is just helping you mass-produce look-alike content.</p>
<h2 id="how-do-you-use-ai-to-make-social-content-that-doesn-t-read-l">How do you use AI to make social content that doesn't read like AI?</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Write a one-page brand point-of-view card.</strong> List facts only you can state: the craftsmanship standards you hold, tests you've run, mistakes you've learned from, the three questions customers ask most. This is the part AI can't write and competitors can't copy.</li>
<li><strong>Build an exclusive asset library.</strong> Factory-floor footage, the sampling process, quality-control records, customer service conversations and buyers' reviews in their own words (used with permission), all filed in one place so you can pull them anytime.</li>
<li><strong>Put at least one exclusive asset into every prompt.</strong> Have AI build the post around one specific fact, not "write a post about our product."</li>
<li><strong>Rewrite for US context instead of translating.</strong> First decide which question the post answers for US users, then have AI write it the way they talk.</li>
<li><strong>Run a sameness check before publishing.</strong> Cover the brand name and ask whether the post would work just as well coming from a competitor. Cut stock openers and hollow closers.</li>
<li><strong>Measure engagement quality, not just reach.</strong> Real questions in the comments, saves and DMs say more about a post's value than likes. Update your point-of-view card monthly based on them.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="what-are-the-three-most-common-traps-for-brands-selling-over">What are the three most common traps for brands selling overseas?</h2>
<p><strong>First, translating Chinese drafts directly.</strong> Sentence-by-sentence translation keeps the promotional tone and Chinese word order, and US users spot it instantly. <strong>Second, chasing trends that don't fit.</strong> Someone else's viral template often falls flat with your category and audience; instead of chasing every trend, join only the conversations that genuinely relate to your product and audience. <strong>Third, fully automated publishing with nobody checking.</strong> Efficiency goes up, but one factual error or badly timed post can undo months of work.</p>

== faq ==
Q: Will LinkedIn delete AI-generated posts outright?
A: Based on public information, LinkedIn mainly identifies AI slop and reduces its distribution, judging whether content is generic, repetitive or lacking insight. According to Social Media Today, its classifier covers every post distributed beyond a member's direct connections, with 94% precision, and LinkedIn previously reported that views of AI slop have fallen by 40%.
Q: Can I have AI translate my Chinese drafts into English and post them?
A: It's fine as a starting point, but don't post the result as is. Sentence-by-sentence translation often keeps Chinese phrasing habits and a promotional tone, and US users notice right away. A better approach is to have AI rewrite around the questions and use cases US users care about, then have someone who knows the local market check the tone, wording and compliance.
Q: How many posts a day do I need on overseas social media?
A: There's no universal answer; frequency should follow quality. For small brands selling overseas, rather than several generic posts a day, make sure every post carries one specific piece of information: a craftsmanship detail, a real question or a customer story. Pick a pace you can sustain, then adjust based on comments, saves, DMs and other engagement data.
Q: How can I tell whether a piece of content is generic?
A: Try a simple test: cover the brand name. Would this post work just as well coming from any competitor? If so, it lacks exclusive information. Also check whether the opening line mirrors popular posts in your industry, whether it leans on one-size-fits-all phrases, and whether the comments contain only emojis and no real discussion.
