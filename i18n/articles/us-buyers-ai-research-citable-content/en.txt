<p>US buyers are starting to use AI for sourcing research, so what content should an exporter prepare for AI to cite? The short answer: <strong>turn the questions buyers ask most into answers AI can lift directly, and put them on your own website</strong>. Specifically, six kinds: company entity information, product spec sheets, FAQs, verifiable proof, selection guides and a consistent reputation across platforms.</p><p>This is getting urgent. <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/ai-assisted-shopping-to-rise-130-during-holidays-adobe-says" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Bloomberg</a> reported that Adobe expects traffic to US retail sites from AI assistants and shopping agents to grow 130% year over year this holiday season; in the same forecast, total online sales grow only 6.7%, traffic from paid search 4% and traffic from social media 17%. <a href="https://www.modernretail.co/operations/the-rise-of-the-ai-shelf/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Modern Retail</a> calls this shift the "AI shelf": people simply ask ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini what to buy.</p><h2 id="are-us-buyers-really-using-ai-to-find-suppliers">Are US buyers really using AI to find suppliers?</h2><p>Start with the consumer data. <a href="https://searchengineland.com/beauty-consumers-google-search-493828" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Search Engine Land</a> covered a Stella Rising survey finding that only 43% of beauty shoppers start looking for products on Google; the rest begin on Instagram, YouTube, TikTok and AI tools. 59.5% of respondents use AI at some point during their search, and among respondents in their 20s, the share starting on Google and the share starting with AI tools were both 24%.</p><p>That's consumer data, not procurement data. But the people making sourcing decisions are the same people: comparing prices on a work laptop by day, asking ChatGPT on their phone at night. Picture a sourcing manager in Ohio typing into an AI tool: "Which Chinese factories make FDA-compliant food-grade silicone kitchenware with an MOQ under 1,000 units?" (MOQ means minimum order quantity.) The AI returns three to five names with reasons. If you're not on that list, your Alibaba.com storefront and your Canton Fair booth can't help you in that moment.</p><p>Modern Retail also describes an experiment: tech writer Deana Burke invented a natural deodorant brand to test whether AI would recommend it, and it worked. That shows AI recommendations are shaped by what's published online. A legitimate business obviously shouldn't invent anything; it should write down the true facts clearly. Otherwise AI can only borrow someone else's description of you, or leave you out altogether.</p><h2 id="what-are-seo-aeo-and-geo-and-why-do-they-matter-for-exporter">What are SEO, AEO and GEO, and why do they matter for exporters?</h2><ul><li><strong>SEO (search engine optimization)</strong>: getting Google to rank your pages near the top of search results so buyers click through. Export websites have been doing this homework for years.</li><li><strong>AEO (answer engine optimization)</strong>: getting your content shown directly as "the answer," such as a featured snippet or AI Overview at the top of Google, so buyers see your words without clicking.</li><li><strong>GEO (generative engine optimization)</strong>: getting AI tools like ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Copilot to mention and cite your company and products when they generate an answer.</li></ul><p>The three don't replace each other; they build on each other. SEO gets you found, AEO gets your words used as the answer, and GEO puts you on AI's shortlist. They share the same foundation: clear, specific, verifiable content.</p><h2 id="why-are-buyers-relying-more-on-ai-to-narrow-the-field">Why are buyers relying more on AI to narrow the field?</h2><p>Psychology's <strong>choice overload theory</strong> says that when there are too many options, people not only find it harder to decide, they're also more likely to put it off or give up. Search a common category on Alibaba.com and you get hundreds or thousands of suppliers who all look alike. Faced with that, asking AI for a shortlist first is the most natural move in the world.</p><p>That makes AI the first filter in sourcing, and only a few names survive it. On page two of Google, you still had a chance of being found. In an AI answer, if you're not on the list, you don't exist.</p><p>Exporters face their own choice overload: TikTok, LinkedIn, your own site, trade shows, marketplace ads, Google Ads, GEO. Someone always says each one is a must. The result is often dabbling in everything and doing nothing well. The fix is subtraction: first get the content on the checklist below right.</p><h2 id="what-content-is-ai-most-likely-to-cite-a-checklist">What content is AI most likely to cite? The difference isn't writing skill; it's information density.</p><h2 id="six-steps-to-prepare-citable-content-from-scratch">Six steps to prepare citable content from scratch</h2><ol><li><strong>List the questions buyers have actually asked.</strong> Go through the past few months of inquiry emails and trade-show notes and list the questions that keep coming up. That's your content plan.</li><li><strong>Give yourself an AI checkup first.</strong> In ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google's AI Mode, ask five to ten questions the way a buyer would, and note which companies appear in the answers and whether you're one of them.</li><li><strong>Fill out your "About us" and spec pages.</strong> Move information hidden in images and PDFs into web-page text, and don't leave out company name, address, certifications, MOQ or lead time.</li><li><strong>Write one FAQ or selection guide for each core category.</strong> Answer the question in the first two or three sentences, then add detail and evidence, so AI can lift the answer directly.</li><li><strong>Keep details consistent across platforms.</strong> Your company name, address and main categories should match on your site, Alibaba.com, your LinkedIn company page and your Google Business Profile, so each one backs up the others.</li><li><strong>Recheck every month.</strong> Ask AI the same set of questions again and record how often you're mentioned and whether the description is accurate.</li></ol><p>If your team doesn't have time to do this by hand every month, a tool can help. The Search Engine Land piece makes the point that platforms aren't replacing one another; they're dividing up the work. After discovering a product on TikTok, 72% of consumers go on to search for it on Google, and only 7% buy directly on TikTok. In the author's framing, TikTok answers whether a product works, and Google answers whether it can be trusted.</p><p>The same division of labor exists in export sales. LinkedIn and YouTube prove you're a real, professional factory; your website answers questions about specs, certifications and lead times; AI pulls it all together into a shortlist. The article also warns that social and GEO aren't an either-or choice. Cutting social to fund GEO tears down one of the main ways buyers verify you.</p>

== faq ==
Q: We already have an Alibaba.com store. Do we still need our own website?
A: We'd recommend it. Marketplace storefronts use a standard layout and limit what you can write, so it's hard to show how you differ from competitors. On your own independent site, you can publish full specs, certifications, FAQs and selection guides, and the content belongs to you. The best approach is to keep both consistent so they back each other up, not to pick one.
Q: Can AI cite product pages we mass-produce with AI?
A: What matters isn't who writes the page but whether it holds specific, verifiable information. If mass-generated pages are just reworded generic descriptions that look like everyone else's, AI has no reason to cite you. Using AI for efficiency is fine, but every page needs real specs, certificate numbers and answers to common questions, reviewed by someone who knows the product.
Q: How long before AI starts mentioning us?
A: There's no standard timeline, and it varies widely by AI tool and product category. A more reliable approach is to fix a set of buyer-style questions and ask each one monthly in ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google's AI Mode, recording whether you're mentioned and whether the description is accurate. Watch the trend, not any single result.
Q: We're short-staffed. Which content should we do first?
A: Start with two things: text-based product spec pages and FAQs. They map directly to the questions sourcing managers ask most, such as certifications, MOQ and lead times, and they're the information AI needs most to answer those questions. Once those are solid, add selection guides and social content.
