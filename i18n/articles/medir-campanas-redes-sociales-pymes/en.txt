== q ==
How do I know if my social media campaigns are actually working?
== a ==
Start by measuring three layers and nothing else: attention (how many people saw the content and for how long), connection (saves, shares, comments and messages per 1,000 people reached) and action (clicks, inquiries or sales, with their cost per result). Compare each campaign with your own average from recent weeks, not with other brands’.
== takeaways ==
- A social media campaign works when it moves the goal you set before launch, not when it piles up more likes.
- Three layers are enough to measure: attention (who really saw it), connection (who saved it, shared it or messaged you) and action (who clicked, inquired or bought).
- Compare each campaign with your own recent average, because other brands’ and industries’ averages rarely look like your business.
- With global ad spending growing 11.9% in 2026, according to the WARC forecast reported by EMARKETER, competing for attention means knowing what works before you spend more.
- Pre-publication scores help filter out weak ideas, but they are an estimate: the real result always has to be confirmed with data.

<p>How do you know whether your social media campaigns are working? Set a goal before you launch, then measure just three things: did people actually see it, did they connect with it, and did they do something afterward. Every other metric is context. With a spreadsheet and fifteen minutes a week, you can decide what to repeat, what to tweak and what to switch off.</p><p>You’ll need that habit more and more. According to <a href="https://www.emarketer.com/content/global-ad-spending-rises-11-9--market-beats-earlier-forecasts" rel="noopener" target="_blank">EMARKETER</a>, which published the figures on October 8, WARC has raised its forecast: global ad spending will grow 11.9% in 2026, to $1.34 trillion, up from the 11.5% it projected in June. EMARKETER also expects spending on AI advertising to climb from about $32 billion this year to more than $68 billion in 2030. With more advertisers fighting for the same attention, guessing keeps getting more expensive.</p><h2 id="what-does-it-mean-for-a-social-media-campaign-to-work">What does it mean for a social media campaign to “work”?</h2><p>It depends on why you launched it. A campaign to get known in your city isn’t measured the same way as one to sell a seasonal promotion. The most common mistake is launching without a goal and then, at the end, hunting for whichever metric looks best in the report.</p><p>Before you post, write one sentence: “This campaign works if it gets X in Y weeks.” For example: “40 messages from new customers in a month” or “1 in every 50 people who see the ad visits the website.” These are examples, not benchmarks: your number will depend on your own track record.</p><h2 id="the-only-three-metrics-you-need-attention-connection-and-act">The only three metrics you need: attention, connection and action</h2><p>Sort everything Instagram, Facebook, TikTok or LinkedIn shows you into three layers. Pick one main metric per layer and ignore the rest until you’ve mastered these.</p><div class="tw"><table><thead><tr><th>Layer</th><th>What it tells you</th><th>Suggested main metric</th><th>Warning sign</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Attention</td><td>Whether people stopped to look</td><td>Reach, plus average watch time or completion rate</td><td>Lots of reach, but almost nobody gets past the first few seconds</td></tr><tr><td>Connection</td><td>Whether they cared about what they saw</td><td>Saves, shares, comments and messages per 1,000 people reached</td><td>Plenty of likes, but no saves or messages</td></tr><tr><td>Action</td><td>Whether they did something useful for your business</td><td>Clicks, inquiries, bookings or sales, and cost per result</td><td>Cheap clicks that never turn into leads or sales</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>The connection layer is the one people forget most, and the one that best predicts the other two. A save means “this is useful to me”; a message means “I want to talk to you.” A like costs one tap and says almost nothing.</p><h2 id="why-are-likes-so-misleading">Why are likes so misleading?</h2><p>Performance measurement theory studies how the indicators you choose end up shaping what you do. The idea fits in one line: what gets measured gets chased. If likes are your star metric, you’ll end up posting what the algorithm likes, not what brings you customers.</p><p>That’s why it pays to measure the heart, not just the numbers: look for signs that someone cared about your post enough to save it, send it to a friend or message you. Those are numbers too, but they tell you something human.</p><p>A hypothetical example: a bike shop posts a funny reel that gets three thousand likes, and a maintenance tip that stalls at four hundred. If the second one brings in twenty saves and five messages asking about the annual tune-up, that’s the one that sells.</p><h2 id="how-to-build-your-measurement-sheet-in-30-minutes">How to build your measurement sheet in 30 minutes</h2><ol><li><strong>Write the campaign goal in one sentence,</strong> with a number and a deadline.</li><li><strong>Pick one metric per layer</strong> from the table above. Three columns, nothing more, to start.</li><li><strong>Calculate your baseline.</strong> Average your last ten to twelve posts or your last campaign. That’s your benchmark, not other brands’.</li><li><strong>Tag your links.</strong> Add UTM parameters (short tags at the end of a URL that tell your analytics tool which campaign each visit came from) to every link you share.</li><li><strong>Ask “How did you hear about us?”</strong> on your form, at the register or when you reply on WhatsApp. Social media influences sales that no analytics tool records.</li><li><strong>Review for fifteen minutes every Monday.</strong> Write down the week’s numbers next to your baseline.</li><li><strong>Make one of three calls:</strong> scale what beats your average, tweak what lands close to it and switch off what has been below it for three weeks.</li></ol><h2 id="how-do-i-calculate-my-social-media-return-without-overcompli">How do I calculate my social media return without overcomplicating it?</h2><p>For paid campaigns, use two formulas. ROAS (return on ad spend) is what you bring in for every unit spent on ads: attributed sales divided by ad spend. ROI (return on investment) goes a step further and subtracts costs: (margin generated − total cost) ÷ total cost.</p><p>Count your own time, or your content creator’s, in total cost. An organic campaign isn’t free if it eats ten hours a month.</p><p>A hypothetical example — euros, dollars or pesos, it doesn’t matter: you spend 300 on ads and they generate 1,200 in sales. Your ROAS is 4. If those sales leave you 480 in margin and you add 100 for design time, your ROI is (480 − 400) ÷ 400 = 0.2, or 20%. Profitable, but a lot less than the ROAS suggested.</p><h2 id="can-you-tell-whether-a-post-will-work-before-you-publish-it">Can you tell whether a post will work before you publish it?</h2><p>Not with certainty. With more information than gut feel, yes. Some models score a concept before it goes live by comparing it with patterns from large volumes of content: which kinds of hook, format or message tend to drive more clicks or engagement.</p><p>That score is a pre-launch estimate, not a guarantee. It helps you drop weak ideas and prioritize promising ones before you spend; then your Monday sheet confirms or corrects it. That’s what <a href="/#social">ToucHigh’s Social Media Manager Autopilot</a> does, for example: it gives every post a predicted engagement score before scheduling it.</p><p>What matters is the order: evidence first, budget second. With more competition for attention, every well-measured campaign is money you don’t throw away.</p>

== faq ==
Q: What’s a good engagement rate on social media?
A: It depends on the platform, the industry, the account size and the format, so generic figures don’t help much. The most useful benchmark is your own average over your last ten to twelve posts. If a campaign clearly beats it on saves, shares or messages, it’s going well; if only likes go up, check the action layer before celebrating.
Q: What’s the difference between ROAS and ROI?
A: ROAS measures how much revenue you bring in for every unit spent on ads: attributed sales divided by ad spend. ROI subtracts all costs, including product cost and team time, and tells you whether you actually made money. A campaign can have a high ROAS and a low ROI if your margins are thin or the content was expensive to produce.
Q: How often should I review my campaign metrics?
A: A fifteen-minute weekly review is enough for most small businesses. Checking the data every day tempts you to change things before the algorithm has had time to learn, while checking once a month lets a failing campaign run too long. Before you switch something off or scale it up, wait until you have two or three weeks of data.
Q: Do predictive scores guarantee that a post will work?
A: No. A pre-launch score compares your concept with patterns observed in large volumes of content and estimates how likely it is to drive clicks or engagement. It’s useful for filtering out weak ideas and prioritizing strong ones before you spend, but the real result depends on timing, audience and competition. That’s why you always need to confirm it with data.
