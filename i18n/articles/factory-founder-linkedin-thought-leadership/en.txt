== body ==
<p>Should a Chinese factory owner build a personal brand on LinkedIn? Yes, and the sooner the better. But what you build should be thought leadership, meaning you keep sharing your professional judgment about your industry in public, not reposting your product photos in a new place. Before they send an inquiry, overseas buyers increasingly check who this factory is and whether the owner knows the business. The owner’s LinkedIn profile is the first booth they visit.</p>

<p>Two news stories make this more urgent. According to <a href="https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-exec-offers-insight-into-the-battle-against-ai-slop/832576/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Social Media Today</a> on October 8, LinkedIn VP of Engineering Tim Jurka explained how the platform spots “AI slop”: its classifiers now cover every post distributed beyond a member’s direct connections, with 94% precision, and the “report AI slop” button launched in August was used by more than 1 million people in under three weeks. A day earlier, <a href="https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/ai-favors-brands-with-an-active-social-media-presence/832450/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Social Media Today</a> reported on SE Ranking research finding that 67.66% of Google AI Overview answers include links to social platforms. On one side, the platform is demoting cookie-cutter content; on the other, AI search is citing active social accounts. Owners with real opinions stand right where those two forces meet.</p>

<h2 id="how-do-overseas-buyers-find-suppliers-now">How do overseas buyers find suppliers now?</h2>

<p>For more than a decade, export factories in China have won customers three main ways: the Canton Fair in Guangzhou and overseas trade shows, inquiries through Alibaba.com (Alibaba’s international B2B marketplace), and Google search plus their own website. All three still work, but the buyer’s first step is changing. Many procurement managers now start by asking ChatGPT, Perplexity or Google’s AI Overviews which factories can make a given product, get a shortlist, and then verify it at trade shows and on platforms.</p>

<p>Three terms often get mixed up here. SEO, search engine optimization, aims to rank your pages high in Google’s list of links. AEO, answer engine optimization, aims to have AI quote your content directly as the answer. GEO, generative engine optimization, aims to get you mentioned and recommended in generative answers from ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews.</p>

<p>LinkedIn matters for all three. According to SE Ranking’s data, 48.58% of Google AI Mode answers to non-branded queries include social links, compared with just 8.4% of ChatGPT answers. So an owner’s posts aren’t only for followers; they may become material Google’s AI cites when it answers procurement questions. But AI tools differ widely in how much they cite social media, so don’t bet everything on one platform.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-an-owner-s-linkedin-worth-more-than-a-trade-show-busi">Why is an owner’s LinkedIn worth more than a trade show business card?</h2>

<p>Sociology offers social capital theory: relationships are a form of capital in themselves, and trust, information and opportunities flow along them. But they only create value while the relationship stays alive. Of the 200 business cards you collect at a trade show, most are sitting in a drawer three months later, because the other person remembers a booth number, not you.</p>

<p>Thought leadership keeps the relationship alive after the show. Imagine a German buyer chatted with you for five minutes at a trade show last month. This month he sees your LinkedIn post on why you turned down an order priced below cost, and next month he sees you break down how new EU rules affect packaging materials. His impression shifts from “a Chinese supplier” to “a factory owner who knows the business and tells it straight.”</p>

<p>Networking is about genuine connection, not collecting business cards. B2B orders are large and partnerships run long, so what buyers fear most is choosing the wrong supplier. Building up trust in their minds ahead of time is worth more than handing out another hundred cards at a show.</p>

<h2 id="linkedin-is-cracking-down-on-ai-slop-how-should-factory-owne">LinkedIn is cracking down on AI slop. How should factory owners post?</h2>

<p>LinkedIn defines AI slop plainly: generic, repetitive posts with no real insight. According to Social Media Today, LinkedIn uses large models to discover new slop patterns, then trains smaller models to spot them quickly. AI agents apply rules to judge whether a post is selling, merely showing off results, or timely, and send uncertain cases to human reviewers. LinkedIn had earlier reported that views of AI slop have fallen 40%.</p>

<p>For factory owners, three kinds of content will lose out more and more: ad posts with a product photo and “inquiries welcome”; holiday greetings and “Happy Friday” filler; and one-click AI “industry insights” that fit any industry once you swap the name.</p>

<p>On the flip side, the algorithm and buyers both welcome what only you can write: real trade-offs on the factory floor, cost and lead-time breakdowns with specific numbers, and your read on where the industry is going. Using AI to polish your English is fine. What matters is whether the opinion is yours.</p>

<h2 id="how-do-you-start-from-zero-a-four-week-starter-checklist">How do you start from zero? A four-week starter checklist</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Fix your profile first.</strong> Use a photo of the owner, and write a headline that says what problem you solve for which buyers, such as “Head of a factory making custom silicone products for North American kitchenware brands.” Your company’s English name must match your website, Alibaba.com store and trade show materials exactly.</li>
<li><strong>List 20 questions buyers often ask.</strong> Go through your sales team’s inquiry records and trade show notes. MOQ (minimum order quantity), sampling time, certifications, why your quote is higher than others’: all ready-made topics.</li>
<li><strong>Post 2 to 3 times a week.</strong> The owner spends half an hour a week talking through ideas, a sales or marketing colleague writes them up in English, and the owner gives a final read so the tone and judgment are their own.</li>
<li><strong>Take the comments seriously.</strong> Reply to every comment. Spend 10 minutes a day leaving substantive comments on posts by target buyers and trade media; this often builds connections faster than your own posts do.</li>
<li><strong>Repurpose what works.</strong> Expand posts that land well into articles on your website to support SEO and GEO, and film suitable content as YouTube videos or TikTok clips, so buyers find the same you on every platform.</li>
<li><strong>Review once a month.</strong> Look at profile visits, direct messages, new follows from target buyers, and whether any inquiry mentions having seen your posts.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="what-if-the-owner-has-no-time-or-weak-english">What if the owner has no time or weak English?</h2>

<p>This is the most common worry, but it’s no reason to give up. Thought leadership is about the ideas, not the prose. The owner talks, the team writes: that split works for many factories. What doesn’t work is handing the whole account to AI for mass production, which lands you right in the category LinkedIn is demoting.</p>

<p>If no one on your team writes natural English, a tool can make the write-up step easier. For example, ToucHigh’s <a href="/#social">Social Media Manager Autopilot</a> writes native US-English posts and articles in your brand voice and gives each piece a predicted engagement score before it’s scheduled, so the owner can approve them one by one. With or without a tool, hold one line: the opinions, examples and numbers come from the owner.</p>

<p>Finally, set realistic expectations. Trust on LinkedIn builds by the quarter, so don’t expect big orders after ten posts. Keep at it for a quarter, then use inquiry quality and the number of conversations to decide whether to invest more.</p>

== faq ==
Q: Should a factory use the owner's personal profile or the company page on LinkedIn?
A: Both, but put the weight on the owner's profile. Person-to-person connection feels more natural on LinkedIn than a brand page, and buyers would rather talk to a specific person. The company page shows credentials, product lines and contact details; the personal profile shares opinions. Link the two, and keep the company's English name and URL consistent with your website and Alibaba.com store.
Q: Will LinkedIn limit my reach if I use AI to write posts?
A: What matters isn't whether you used AI but whether the content is generic, repetitive and insight-free. LinkedIn defines those posts as AI slop and uses classifiers to identify them. Using AI to polish your English or tidy the structure is usually fine. If the opinions, examples and numbers are all AI-made and would fit any company with the name swapped, the post is likely to be demoted.
Q: How long does it take for LinkedIn to bring inquiries?
A: There's no fixed timeline; it depends on your category, how concentrated your buyers are and the quality of your content. A safer approach is to evaluate by the quarter: in the first month or two, watch follows, comments and messages from target buyers, then check whether inquiries mention your posts. Judging by likes on individual posts is easy to get wrong.
Q: Besides LinkedIn, should factories also be on YouTube and TikTok?
A: If you have the capacity, yes, but don't launch everything at once. LinkedIn suits owners sharing opinions and connecting with procurement teams. YouTube suits factory footage and process explainers, and according to SE Ranking's research it is also the source Google AI Overviews cite most. TikTok suits brands selling to end consumers. Get LinkedIn steady first, then adapt posts that land well into video.
