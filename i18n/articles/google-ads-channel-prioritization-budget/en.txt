== q ==
My budget is limited, and I want to advertise on Amazon, Google, Meta and TikTok. How should I split my ad budget across platforms to get the best return?
== a ==
Don't split it evenly. Put most of your budget on the one channel that already brings in steady orders, until each extra dollar clearly returns less; then test a second channel with a small slice. Google's new high/low channel priorities follow the same logic: fund high-priority channels first, then cap what's left for low-priority ones. Review marginal ROAS (return on ad spend) every two weeks and cut channels that miss.
== takeaways ==
- The thinner you spread a budget, the harder it is for any channel to collect enough data for its algorithm to learn, so an even split is often the most expensive split.
- Google Ads' new channel prioritization spends on high-priority channels first, then sends the remaining budget to low-priority channels up to a maximum share you set, across Search, Shopping, Performance Max and Demand Gen campaigns.
- Whether to add budget depends on the marginal return of the next dollar, not on the account's average ROAS.
- Before February 2027, check any Search campaigns that have the Display Network turned on; to keep things as they are, set Display as low priority with a 50% budget cap.

<h2 id="how-should-you-split-ad-budget-across-platforms-the-short-an">How should you split ad budget across platforms? The short answer</h2><p>Many cross-border sellers get stuck on the same question: there's only so much money, and you want to be on Amazon, Google, Meta and TikTok, so how do you split it? The answer: <strong>run fewer channels, fully fund one main channel that brings in steady orders, then use a small slice to test the next one</strong>. An even split looks safe, but it's often the most expensive way to spend.</p><p>Google just built this idea into its product. <a href="https://searchengineland.com/google-ads-introduces-channel-prioritization-and-budget-allocation-controls-495946" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Search Engine Land</a> reported on October 14 that Google Ads is rolling out two controls, channel prioritization and budget allocation. Advertisers can mark channels as high or low priority; high-priority channels get budget first, and what's left flows to low-priority ones. At the same time, starting in February 2027, Google will retire the existing spend allocation settings for Search campaigns that have the Display Network turned on.</p><h2 id="what-exactly-did-google-change">What exactly did Google change?</h2><p>According to Search Engine Land, the new controls cover four campaign types (Search, Shopping, Performance Max and Demand Gen) and will roll out globally over the coming months. No specific date has been announced.</p><ul><li><strong>Channel prioritization:</strong> Mark each channel "high" or "low." Google spends on high-priority channels first, then distributes the remaining budget to low-priority ones.</li><li><strong>Budget allocation:</strong> Set a maximum share of daily budget for low-priority channels. Google describes this as best effort, not a guaranteed hard cap.</li><li><strong>Channel scope:</strong> For Search campaigns, that means Google Search, Search Partners and the Display Network. Performance Max and Demand Gen also include Google-owned channels such as YouTube, Gmail and Discover.</li></ul><p>Two points deserve attention. First, if you bid with target cost per acquisition (tCPA) or target return on ad spend (tROAS) and the campaign isn't short on budget, channel prioritization alone may not change much. Second, Search campaigns with the Display Network on will be affected by the February 2027 change. The report notes that if you want to keep your current allocation, you can set Display as low priority with a 50% budget cap.</p><p>For small sellers, the point isn't the new switch in the dashboard. It's that even Google is acknowledging that spraying money across every channel at once isn't the best answer.</p><h2 id="why-does-spreading-budget-thinner-make-results-worse">Why does spreading budget thinner make results worse?</h2><p>The economics behind it is <strong>diminishing marginal returns</strong>: keep adding money to the same channel, and at first each extra dollar brings in plenty of orders. Past a certain point, the same dollar brings in fewer and fewer.</p><p>Many sellers conclude, "Then I'll open more channels to spread the risk," and miss the other half of the picture: every channel has a startup threshold. The ad algorithm needs enough conversion data to learn whom to target, and creatives need enough impressions before you can tell good from bad. Split a budget four ways and all four channels may stall at "not enough data, algorithm hasn't learned yet." Every platform in the account is spending, and none of them is really working.</p><p>So the real question isn't "How much does each platform get?" It's "<strong>Does the next dollar on my main channel still return more than a new channel would?</strong>" As long as the answer is yes, the money should stay on your main channel. When too many options create chaos, less is more.</p><h2 id="a-four-step-plan-from-one-main-channel-to-many">A four-step plan: from one main channel to many</h2><p>Say you're an Amazon seller or run an independent DTC site, you're just starting out in the US, and your monthly ad budget is $3,000. Work in this order:</p><ol><li><strong>Pick your main channel.</strong> Amazon sellers usually start with on-Amazon ads, because buyer intent is clearest there; independent-site sellers usually start with Google Search or Shopping ads. The main channel gets the lion's share, and the goal is steady orders, not impressions.</li><li><strong>Find the point where more money stops paying off.</strong> Raise the main channel's budget one notch at a time (say, 15% to 20%) and watch marginal ROAS for one to two weeks. ROAS, or return on ad spend, is ad-driven sales divided by ad spend; marginal ROAS looks only at the extra sales from the extra spend. When it falls clearly below the account average, the main channel is close to saturated.</li><li><strong>Test a second channel with a small share.</strong> Once the main channel nears saturation, set aside a small slice to test an interest-based channel like Meta or TikTok. Write down the test period and the passing bar before you start.</li><li><strong>Reallocate by marginal return every two weeks.</strong> Promote channels that perform to "high priority," and demote or shut down those that keep missing. Inside a Google account, you can enforce this directly with the new high/low priorities and percentage caps.</li></ol><p>A hypothetical example: at $500 a week, Google Shopping ads bring in $2,000 in sales, a ROAS of 4. Raise the budget to $600, and sales become $2,200. The account's average ROAS still looks fine at 3.7, but the extra $100 brought in only $200 more, a marginal ROAS of 2. If your break-even ROAS is 2.5, that $100 is actually losing money and would be better spent testing a second channel.</p><p>The 15% to 20% and the "small slice" are starting points; set your real numbers based on your own margin and average order value. What matters is the order: go deep first, then go wide.</p><h2 id="what-role-does-each-channel-play-in-the-mix">What role does each channel play in the mix?</h2><p>The table below isn't a fixed ratio. It helps you decide whether each channel should be on right now, and what to watch once it is.</p><div class="tw"><table><thead><tr><th>Channel</th><th>Role in the mix</th><th>When to add it</th><th>Signs to demote or pause it</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Amazon ads</td><td>Capture existing purchase intent</td><td>Listing reviews and conversion rate are stable</td><td>Orders don't rise when you raise bids, and ACoS (ad spend as a share of ad sales) keeps climbing</td></tr><tr><td>Google Search / Shopping</td><td>Main source of high-intent traffic for an independent site</td><td>Product pages, checkout and conversion tracking are all in place</td><td>Clicks on core keywords keep getting pricier, and more budget only buys broad, low-intent traffic</td></tr><tr><td>Google Display Network / YouTube</td><td>Extra reach and remarketing</td><td>Search is near saturation and you have usable image or video creative</td><td>The low-priority budget gets spent with almost no assisted conversions</td></tr><tr><td>Meta (Facebook / Instagram)</td><td>Reach new interest-based audiences</td><td>You have a few strong creatives and can ride out a learning period</td><td>Creative fatigue, rising frequency, and cost per purchase that keeps climbing</td></tr><tr><td>TikTok</td><td>Content-led discovery and testing for breakout products</td><td>You can keep producing short videos, and someone on the team understands content</td><td>High views but low clicks, or results propped up entirely by creators' organic reach</td></tr></tbody></table></div><h2 id="when-should-you-cut-a-channel">When should you cut a channel?</h2><p>Reluctance to switch things off is the most common money leak in multichannel advertising. Before any test, write down three exit rules:</p><ul><li>At the end of the test period (say, four weeks), marginal ROAS is still below your break-even line.</li><li>After two rounds of new creative, cost per purchase still hasn't come down.</li><li>Tracking for the channel is incomplete, so you can't say how many orders it actually drove.</li></ul><p>Hit any one of them, and move the budget back to your main channel or hand it to the next candidate. Shutting off a channel isn't failure. It just tells you that money is worth more somewhere else.</p><h2 id="no-one-watches-the-dashboards-every-day-how-do-you-enforce-b">No one watches the dashboards every day. == faq ==
Q: When can I use Google's channel prioritization?
A: According to Search Engine Land, it will roll out globally over the coming months for Search, Shopping, Performance Max and Demand Gen campaigns; no specific date has been announced. Separately, starting in February 2027, Google will retire the existing spend allocation settings for Search campaigns with the Display Network turned on, so check those campaigns early.
Q: My budget is only a few hundred dollars. Should I run several platforms at once?
A: Usually not. The smaller the budget, the more it should be concentrated in one channel so the ad algorithm collects conversion data quickly. A few hundred dollars split across three or four platforms often leaves each one stuck in its learning period, with no real read on results. Get steady orders from one channel first, then consider a second.
Q: What's the difference between marginal ROAS and average ROAS?
A: Average ROAS is total sales divided by total ad spend, and it shows how efficient you've been overall. Marginal ROAS looks at how much extra sales the extra spend brought in. An account's average ROAS can look healthy while the last dollars you added are already losing money. When deciding whether to add budget, look at marginal ROAS.
Q: If I set a cap for low-priority channels, will Google strictly enforce it?
A: Not necessarily. Search Engine Land notes that budget allocation caps are best effort: Google will try to stay within the share you set but doesn't guarantee a hard cap. After you set it up, keep checking each channel's actual share of spend and adjust when you see it drift.
