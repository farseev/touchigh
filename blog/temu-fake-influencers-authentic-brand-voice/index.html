Authentic Content Marketing: What Temu's Fake-Influencer Purge Teaches SMEs

Temu's fake-influencer network collapsed within weeks of exposure. Here's how small brands build an authentic voice that compounds instead: customer words, clear opinions, real proof.

ToucHigh Research TeamPublished October 16, 20266 min readEnglish
Also available in简体中文
“
The question behind this article

How can I make sure my content reflects my brand's voice and connects with my audience authentically?

Short answerAuthentic content comes from what only your brand has: your customers' own words, a clear point of view and proof you can show. Write three voice rules, pick three topics you can back up, and filter every trend by whether it helps your customers. Temu's fake-influencer purge shows borrowed reach can vanish in weeks, while earned trust compounds.

Key takeaways

  • Fortune reported that Temu scaled back a Meta partnership-ad network worth close to $1 billion after researchers judged about 73 of its top 100 influencer pages probably fake.
  • Herd behavior pushes brands to copy trending formats, which is why so much "authentic" social content ends up looking identical.
  • An authentic brand voice is specific, consistent and provable: your customers' words, one clear opinion per post and real proof.
  • Vet creators like hires by checking comment quality, personal content and audience data, and start with a single paid test.
  • Staying on-voice every week is a system: a shared voice doc, weekly batching and one approval question.

How do you make content that sounds like your brand and actually connects, instead of looking like every other post in the feed? Stop borrowing other people's formulas. Build your posts from what only you have (your customers' words, your opinions, your proof) and run every trend through one filter: would our customers miss this if we didn't post it?

This fall delivered the cautionary tale. Fortune reported that Temu scaled back a network of Meta partnership ads worth close to $1 billion that ran through influencer accounts, many of them judged likely fake. Researchers at Online Risk Labs estimated that about 73 of the 100 influencer pages carrying the most Temu ads were probably fake. After Fortune's first story on August 31, weekly partnership ads on the network fell from 115,114 to 64,008.

What happened with Temu's influencer network?

Partnership ads are a Meta format: a creator posts content that tags a brand, and the brand pays to boost that post as an ad. According to Fortune, the creator gets a share of the budget, and Meta said earlier this year the format was on course for a $10 billion annual run rate across Facebook and Instagram.

Online Risk Labs, a Czech research group, studied Temu's activity over 16 months ending in April 2026, with ads aimed at users in the UK and the EU. Fortune reports that the top 100 accounts were involved in more than 1.4 million partnership ads, and that 54 of them stopped running partnership ads almost immediately after the first story. Neither Temu nor Meta responded to Fortune's requests for comment.

The lesson for a small brand isn't "never work with creators." It's that reach built on accounts nobody is really behind can disappear in a week, and that scrutiny of social ad tactics, especially those used by cross-border sellers, is rising.

Why do brands keep copying content that doesn't fit them?

Economists call it herd behavior theory: when we're unsure what works, we copy what everyone else seems to be doing and assume the crowd knows something we don't. On social media, the crowd is very visible. A format takes off, a competitor jumps on it, and copying feels safer than guessing.

The problem is that the herd flattens everyone into the same post. ToucHigh's competitive-content research found that about 87% of social content crowds the same "authentic" formula. You know the type: the behind-the-scenes clip, the founder confessional, the trending audio with a "real talk" caption. When everyone performs spontaneity the same way, it stops reading as authentic.

In a world of trends, authenticity is the rarest currency, because so few brands do the slower work of earning it. Fake reach sits at the far end of the same herd instinct: it's what you get when the goal is to look like you're everywhere instead of being worth listening to somewhere.

What does an authentic brand voice actually look like?

Authentic doesn't mean unpolished. It means specific, consistent and true. Cover your logo, and a regular customer should still be able to guess the post is yours. Here's the difference in practice:

How do you find your brand voice in one afternoon?

You don't need a branding agency. Block three hours on Monday and work through these steps:

  1. Collect 20 real customer sentences. Pull them from reviews, support emails, DMs and sales calls. Highlight the exact phrases people use to describe the problem you solve.
  2. Write three voice rules. Give each one a "we say" and a "we never say." For a hypothetical coffee roaster: "We say 'roasted Tuesday, shipped Wednesday.' We never say 'artisanal.'"
  3. Pick three content pillars you can prove. Choose topics where you have receipts: a process you can film, results you can show, expertise your team really has.
  4. Name your faces. Decide who appears in and signs your posts: the founder, a named team member, customers who agreed to be featured.
  5. Write a one-line trend filter. "Does this trend help us show [pillar] to [customer]?" If not, skip it, however big it is.
  6. Draft ten posts and read them aloud. Cut anything you wouldn't say to a customer standing at your counter.
  7. Review after four weeks. Look at saves, replies and DMs, not just likes. Comments that mention specifics are the clearest sign your voice is landing.

Should small brands still work with influencers?

Yes, but treat a creator like a hire, not a media buy. A small creator whose real audience matches your customers will usually do more for you than a big account with followers you can't verify. Before you pay anyone, check:

  • Do the comments respond to the content specifically, or are they generic praise and emojis?
  • Does the account post about its own life and opinions, or only about sponsored products?
  • Will the creator share audience screenshots from the platform's insights, such as location and age?
  • Can you start with one paid post and judge it on sales or sign-ups before committing to a series?

If you boost creator content through partnership ads, make sure the paid relationship is clearly disclosed. Disclosure costs you nothing and protects the trust you're paying for.

How do you stay authentic when you need to post every week?

This is where most small teams slip. The voice is sharp in month one, then deadlines pile up and posts drift back toward whatever is trending. Consistency is a system problem, not a creativity problem.

Keep a shared voice doc with your rules and customer phrases. Batch-write posts once a week from your pillars. Approve everything against one question: does this sound like us, and does it prove something?

Frequently asked questions

Can I use AI to write social posts and still be authentic?

Yes, as long as the substance comes from you. Authenticity lives in the stories, opinions, customer language and proof behind a post, not in who typed the first draft. Give any AI tool your voice rules and real customer phrases, edit the drafts so they sound like you, and approve every post until you trust the output.

How can I tell if an influencer's audience is fake?

Read the comments: generic praise and emoji strings on every post are a warning sign. Check whether the account shares its own life and opinions or only sponsored products. Ask for screenshots of the platform's audience insights, such as location and age. Then run one paid post and judge it on sales or sign-ups before you commit to more.

What are Meta partnership ads?

Partnership ads let a brand boost a creator's post as a paid ad. The creator publishes content that tags the brand, the brand pays to promote it, and, according to Fortune, the creator receives a share of the budget. Meta said earlier in 2026 that the format was on course for a $10 billion annual run rate across Facebook and Instagram.

How often should a small business post to stay authentic?

Post as often as you can while still saying something true and specific. A few posts a week built on real customer questions and proof will do more for trust than daily filler that chases trends. Pick a cadence you can sustain for six months, batch-write once a week, and only increase frequency when quality holds.
