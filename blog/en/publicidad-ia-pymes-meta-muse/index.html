AI advertising for small businesses: Meta Muse and when not to automate

AI can already propose campaigns for you, but not everything should be handed off. A guide for small businesses that don't know how to run ads: what to automate, what to decide yourself and where to start.

ToucHigh Research TeamPublished October 16, 20267 min readEnglish
Also available in简体中文Español

The question behind this article

I don't know how to make ads: can I let artificial intelligence run my small business's advertising?

Short answerYes, but in pieces. Let AI propose copy, creative variations and budget adjustments, and keep three decisions for yourself: the offer, the maximum budget and final approval. Don't automate if you don't yet track sales or inquiries, if your landing page is broken, or if nobody is going to review what gets published.

Key takeaways

  • According to Social Media Today, Meta launched Muse for small businesses: AI agents that can analyze your content and create promotional campaigns, connected to tools such as Shopify, Canva, Stripe or QuickBooks.
  • Meta says nothing is published, sent or spent through Muse without explicit consent; even so, deciding what access you grant is still your responsibility.
  • AI is useful for generating variations, allocating budget and spotting worn-out ads; the offer, the spending cap and final approval should stay human decisions.
  • Don't automate your ads if you don't yet track sales, bookings or messages: the algorithm optimizes toward what it can see.

Can you let AI run your small business's advertising if you don't know how to make ads? Partly, yes. AI already writes copy, proposes campaigns and spreads a budget with more discipline than a beginner in a hurry. But some decisions shouldn't be handed off, and in some situations automating only helps you lose money faster.

The question comes at a good time. According to Social Media Today, Meta unveiled Muse for small businesses in late September: AI agents that, through connectors to tools such as Shopify, Canva, Stripe, QuickBooks, Slack or Zoom, can analyze your content and create promotional campaigns, review your marketing plan or draft replies to customers. Meta says nothing gets published, sent or spent without the user's explicit consent.

What is Meta Muse and what can it do for your ads?

Muse is Meta's family of AI agents, and the small-business version brings them into running the business. They work through conversation: you ask for something and the agent prepares it using data from your connected tools and from Meta's own tools and analytics.

According to the report, there are four intended uses: analyzing content and creating promotional campaigns; assessing financial performance and flagging risks or opportunities; alerting you to chances to engage customers and drafting replies; and reviewing marketing plans to suggest improvements. Meta is also working on a way to set and track business goals.

What the report doesn't spell out: which countries it will be available in, when, or at what price. And it leaves one question open: how much access to their data small businesses will want to give Meta. The author suggests starting with the lowest-risk uses, such as asking for strategy advice for Facebook and Instagram.

Why can AI level the playing field for small businesses?

A big brand has a media team, designers, analysts and an agency. A small business has the owner, maybe one trusted helper and a free afternoon a week. That's why advertising feels like roulette to so many small businesses: they don't lack judgment, they lack time and specialists.

Resource dependence theory, which comes from the sociology of organizations, helps explain this: whoever controls a resource you need has power over you. For years, small businesses depended on expensive agencies or on their gut. Now AI puts part of that expertise within anyone's reach.

But the dependence moves somewhere else. If everything runs through one platform's agent, your data, your creatives and your decisions stay inside its ecosystem. Shared resources lift everyone only if you stay in control of what matters most: your customer data, your offer and your budget.

When should you not automate your advertising?

Automation amplifies whatever is already there. If the foundation is shaky, AI just helps you fail faster. Hit the brakes if any of these sound like you:

  • You don't track results. Without the Meta pixel, the Google tag or a log of messages and sales, the algorithm optimizes toward cheap clicks, not customers.
  • Your offer isn't clear. No AI can fix a product with no visible price or a service nobody can understand in one sentence.
  • Your landing page is broken. If it loads slowly or has no clear button to buy or contact you, you'll pay for visits that leave.
  • You're in a regulated industry. Health, finance, alcohol and legal services have their own rules: review every ad before it goes out.
  • Nobody will review anything. If you can't spend fifteen minutes a week looking at results, any autopilot will end up going where you don't want it to.

How to start with AI advertising without losing control

  1. Pick one measurable goal. WhatsApp messages, bookings or online sales. One, not three.
  2. Set up tracking before you spend. Make sure every purchase or inquiry is recorded and that you know which ad it came from.
  3. Set a monthly cap. An amount you can lose without it hurting while you learn. Configure it in the platform, not just in your head.
  4. Start with low-risk tasks. Ask the AI for ad ideas, copy and a review of your Facebook and Instagram strategy before giving it access to payments.
  5. Connect tools one at a time. Use the minimum permissions and write down what you granted and when.
  6. Check three numbers every Monday. Cost per result, ROAS (return on ad spend: how much revenue you bring in for every euro, dollar or peso you spend on ads) and frequency (how many times, on average, each person has seen your ad).

A hypothetical example: a natural cosmetics shop on a tight budget starts by asking the AI for five versions of an ad for its best-selling product, approves two and tests them with a fixed monthly cap. Only once the cost per sale settles down does it let the system allocate the budget on its own.

What if I'd rather have someone run it for me?

That's a reasonable choice if you have neither the time nor the desire to learn every platform. What matters is that whoever runs it, a person or software, works by your rules: your goal, your cap and your approval.

Inequality stifles innovation; shared resources lift us all.

Frequently asked questions

Can Meta Muse spend my money without my approval?

According to Meta, as cited by Social Media Today, users stay in control of the agents and nothing is published, sent or spent without explicit consent. Even so, the risk isn't only spend: it's also how much of your business data you share when you connect your store, payments or accounting. Grant minimum permissions and review them regularly.

Is Meta Muse available in Spain and Latin America?

Social Media Today's report on the launch doesn't give countries, rollout dates or pricing. The sensible move is to watch for updates inside your Meta account and not plan your advertising around a tool you don't have yet. In the meantime, the principles in this guide apply to any AI advertising assistant.

How much budget do I need for the AI to learn?

There's no universal number. Automated systems need enough results to learn from, so if your budget only brings in a few sales a month, optimize toward a more frequent action, such as messages or add-to-carts. Start with a cap you can afford to lose while you learn, and raise it only once your cost per result stabilizes.

Does AI replace an advertising agency?

For execution tasks, more and more: copy, variations, bidding and budget allocation. What it doesn't replace is judgment about your business: what to offer, to whom, at what margin and which promises you can keep. For a small business, a sensible combination is AI that executes and a person, you or a specialist, who sets the rules and reviews the results.
