Google Business Profile and social: a local visibility kit for small businesses

If you don't review suggested edits to your Google listing, someone else may end up deciding your hours or your phone number. A practical kit so customers find you, trust you and get in touch.

ToucHigh Research TeamPublished October 16, 20267 min readEnglish
Also available in简体中文Español

The question behind this article

How do I get customers in my area to find me on Google and engage with me more on social media?

Short answerStart with your Google Business Profile, the listing that appears when people search for you on Google and Maps. Keep your name, hours, phone, website and category identical on your site and social accounts, review suggested edits (Google may publish them if you don't respond within four days), reply to reviews and post on social about what customers actually ask.

Key takeaways

  • According to PuroMarketing, if you don't respond within four days to a suggested edit on your Google Business Profile, Google may publish it when other public information backs it up.
  • Your website is one of the references Google uses to back up a detail, so if it shows outdated hours, it works against you.
  • The most sensitive fields on your listing are the closed status, the map pin, the business name, the primary category, the phone number and the website.
  • Social media engagement starts before you post: a customer who finds contradictory details stops trusting you before reading a word of your post.
  • Checking your listing once a month is no longer enough: with a four-day deadline, alerts need a look every day or, at most, every other day.

How do you get customers in your area to find you, and then message you, comment or come through the door? Before you plan your next Reel, look at your Google Business Profile: if the information there is wrong, every bit of effort you put into social media leaks away. Local engagement rests on three things: accurate details, fast replies and content that answers real questions.

There is also a deadline you should know about. On October 7, PuroMarketing explained that, according to Google's updated help documentation, when a user proposes an edit to your profile that needs your review, you have four days to accept or reject it. If you don't respond, Google may publish it as long as other public information backs it up, such as your own website. The article doesn't say whether those are business days.

What changed in Google Business Profile?

Google Business Profile (what many owners still call Google My Business) has accepted suggestions from users for years. What's new is how clear the deadline is, and what that means: your silence no longer stops a change.

According to the article, Google notifies you by email or inside the product itself. On top of that, some user edits can go live without prior review, Google can pull in information from other sources, and in the United States the conversational assistant in Google Maps lets people suggest changes by voice or with a photo of a sign showing your hours.

Not every change carries the same weight. The highest-impact ones, according to the piece, are the "permanently closed" status, the position of your pin on the map, your business name, primary category, phone number and website. And a wrong listing can end up repeated in what AI assistants say about your business.

Why isn't having the data enough to connect with customers?

You know your business better than anyone. Your customer doesn't: they know what they see on Google, what the reviews say and what they find on Instagram or Facebook. Economists call this information asymmetry: one side knows far more than the other, and the side that knows less decides with whatever is at hand.

When you stay quiet, others fill the gap: a user who misremembers your hours, an old directory listing your previous phone number, or a competitor who changes your category. And your metrics won't warn you. The dashboard will tell you how many views your listing got, but not that three people showed up on a Sunday and found the door locked.

That's why real connection starts where the data can't reach: making sure what the customer sees is true, and replying the way a person would, not a system.

How to lock down your Google listing this week

  1. Check who receives the alerts. The article warns that, especially at chains, alerts often go to accounts of people who no longer work there, to former agencies or to generic inboxes nobody opens. Review who manages the profile and remove access that's no longer needed.
  2. Turn on notifications on your phone. With a four-day window, finding out on Friday about an alert sent on Monday is already too late.
  3. Line up your website with your listing. Google uses public information, such as your site, to decide whether a change is backed up. If your website still shows last year's hours, it works against you.
  4. Get ahead of holidays. Special hours are prime territory for well-meaning but wrong suggestions. Post them yourself before someone else does.
  5. Keep a log. A simple spreadsheet with the date, the field that changed and what you decided. That way you spot patterns, such as category changes that keep coming back.
  6. Reply to every review. Thank people for good ones with a specific detail and answer bad ones with a solution. Your next customers read those replies.

What does your Google listing have to do with ChatGPT and other AI assistants?

More than it seems. When someone asks an AI assistant about a business nearby, the answer is built from public information, and your Google listing is one of the most visible pieces of that information. If your hours or phone number are wrong there, the mistake can travel into other answers.

The practical rule is consistency: the same name, address, phone number and hours on your listing, your website, your social accounts and every directory you appear in. The fewer contradictions there are between sources, the less room for error any system that talks about you will have, whether it's a search engine, an assistant or a person comparing options.

What should you post on social media so customers respond?

With your listing in order, social media stops being a shop window and becomes a conversation. The key is to step out of the recycled formula: according to ToucHigh's competitive-content research, about 87% of social content crowds into the same "authentic" formula, which is why so many brands end up sounding alike.

For a local business, specifics usually work better:

  • The questions people ask you at the counter, answered in a Reel or a carousel: "Do you deliver to my neighborhood?", "Do you take cards?", "How long does the repair take?"
  • Changes to your hours or services, posted the same day on your listing and on social.
  • Real people from your team explaining something they do well.
  • Reviews turned into stories, always with the customer's permission.

Measure engagement by what it brings you: messages, calls, bookings and visits, not just likes. Frequently asked questions

What happens if I don't respond to a suggested edit on my Google profile?

According to PuroMarketing, citing Google's help documentation, you have four days to accept or reject an edit that requires your review. If you don't respond, Google may publish it as long as other public information backs it up, such as your website. Google doesn't specify whether those are business days, so it pays to check your alerts every day.

Who can suggest changes to my Google Business Profile?

Any user can suggest edits, from your hours to your category or phone number. According to PuroMarketing's article, some edits go live without prior review, Google can add details from other sources, and in the United States the Google Maps assistant lets people suggest changes by voice or with a photo of a sign. Some suggestions are well-meaning; others are self-interested.

Are Google My Business and Google Business Profile the same thing?

Yes. Google Business Profile is the current name for what was known for years as Google My Business. It's the listing that appears in Search and on Google Maps with your hours, address, phone number, photos and reviews. You manage it by signing in with the account that owns the profile, so make sure that account is still in your company's hands.

How often should a local business post on social media?

There's no magic number. Posting one or two useful pieces a week for months beats a daily streak you abandon after three weeks. Prioritize the questions customers ask you, changes to your hours or services and the faces of your team, and watch which posts lead to messages, calls or bookings, not just reactions.
