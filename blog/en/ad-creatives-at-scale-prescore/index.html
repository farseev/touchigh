AI Ad Creatives: Making Them in Bulk Is Easy, Scoring Them First Saves Money

When a URL is all it takes to generate an ad, making creatives is no longer the hard part; picking the right ones is. Here's a score-first workflow for producing ad creatives in bulk.

ToucHigh Research TeamPublished October 16, 20266 min readEnglish
Also available in简体中文
“
The question behind this article

Is there a way to use AI to automate and simplify ad creative production, and still turn out lots of eye-catching ad images and videos?

Short answerYes. Use AI to produce ad creatives in bulk: list 3–5 selling points and 2–3 audiences and scenarios, combine them into a creative matrix, and have AI produce images, copy and short-video scripts. But don't launch everything. Use a predictive score or a checklist to cut the bottom half before going live, test the rest on small, equal budgets, scale only the winners, and refresh regularly to avoid fatigue.

Key takeaways

  • X and Meta both launched AI ad tools for small businesses in recent weeks, bringing the cost of making a creative close to zero.
  • What holds small sellers back is often not a lack of creativity but the cognitive load of handling too many unfamiliar steps at once.
  • The biggest risk of bulk generation is burning test budget on creatives whose problems are obvious at a glance.
  • Predictive scores are good for ranking creatives and deciding which to test first, but they can't replace real ad tests.

If you want AI to automate your ad creative production and turn out good-looking ads in bulk, the tools are no longer the hard part: paste in a URL and you get images, copy, even a fully built campaign. The hard part is knowing which ads deserve budget before you spend it. A workable approach: break creatives into "selling point × audience × scenario" combinations, let AI generate them in bulk, score them before launch to weed out the obvious losers, test the rest on a small budget, and scale only the few that win.

In the past few weeks, two platforms made the "generate" step practically free. According to Social Media Today, X launched X Lift: an advertiser enters a website link and the system generates a set of image-plus-copy ad options. It can even build the entire campaign, targeting included, so the advertiser only sets the budget. X says its active self-serve advertisers grew 50% year over year in the third quarter of 2026, and the tool is aimed mainly at new small and mid-sized advertisers.

Shortly before that, Social Media Today reported that Meta launched Muse, an AI agent for small businesses that connects to tools such as Shopify, Canva, Stripe and QuickBooks to take on marketing and admin work. Meta says Muse won't post, send anything or spend money without explicit consent.

How far can AI already take ad creative for small sellers?

Going by these two announcements, the platforms' built-in AI can now handle most of the grunt work, from "reading your website" to producing creatives and setting up campaigns. For cross-border sellers who have no design team and aren't confident writing English copy, that is a real cost saving.

Keep three things in mind, though. First, each platform's tools optimize inside its own ecosystem. X Lift is trained on ad data from X, and Meta's tools serve Meta's placements; neither will tell you whether a creative also works on another channel. Second, when everyone can generate ads from the same URL in one click, creative volume will explode, and so will sameness. Third is access. The Social Media Today writer cautions that how useful Muse becomes depends on how much data and access a small business is willing to grant, and suggests starting with low-risk questions such as Facebook and Instagram strategy. Cross-border sellers should be especially careful about granting access to their store back end, payments and ad accounts.

In other words, making creatives is no longer what's valuable. Picking the right ones is.

Why do so many sellers still struggle to make ads that perform, even with these tools?

Plenty of factory owners turned brand sellers carry a default assumption: "Professional video ads need a native-English team and people who can shoot and edit. We can't do that." So they fall back on the old ways: white-background product photos with a spec sheet, or a copy of a competitor's hit ad.

Cognitive load theory from psychology explains what's going on. Working memory is limited, and when a task asks us to juggle too many new elements at once, the brain stalls; we either give up or retreat to what's familiar. Scriptwriting, finding scenes, shooting, editing, English copy, platform specs, audience targeting: stack seven or eight unfamiliar jobs together and any business owner will feel overwhelmed.

So what really holds small sellers back is often not creative ability but imagined complexity. The fix isn't to learn every skill. It's to make the task smaller: make one decision per step, hand the repetitive work to AI, and hand the "will this one work?" guesswork to data and scoring.

What's the biggest risk once you generate creatives in bulk?

Spending real money testing a pile of creatives that never should have gone live. Imagine a seller who generates 60 creatives in one go and gives each a small test budget. Most of that money ends up on creatives with problems you could spot at a glance: no hook in the first three seconds, a muddled selling point, English that obviously reads like a translation.

The second risk is sameness. ToucHigh's competitive-content research found that about 87% of social content crowds into the same "authentic" formula. AI only amplifies that convergence. The same URL and the same prompts naturally produce ads that look alike, which makes them harder to remember in the feed.

How do you build a score-first, launch-second creative workflow?

  1. List 3–5 selling points in US shoppers' words. Instead of "made with premium materials," write a result people can feel, like "no pilling after 50 washes." Back every selling point with evidence, such as test data or real reviews.
  2. Pick 2–3 audiences and use cases. The same storage box gets pitched completely differently to young renters and to families with kids.
  3. Combine them into a creative matrix. Selling point × audience × format (static image, short video, carousel). For example, 4 × 3 × 2 gives you 24 concepts. Write one sentence per concept stating who it's for and what it says.
  4. Have AI generate in bulk. Use the platforms' built-in tools or general image and video generators, but feed every piece the selling point and evidence from step 1 so nothing comes out vague.
  5. Score and filter before launch. Use a predictive score or the checklist below to cut the bottom-ranked half of your concepts. Rewrite low scorers that have a good idea behind them, then score them again.
  6. Test on small, equal budgets. Give the remaining creatives the same budget and the same audience, let them reach enough impressions, then compare click-through rate and cost per conversion. Don't draw conclusions from a day or two of data.
  7. Scale the winners, record the patterns, refresh regularly. Note the selling point, opening and format of each winner as the starting point for your next matrix, and keep backup creatives ready for when fatigue sets in.

No scoring tool? How can you check creatives yourself before launch?

If you don't have a predictive scoring tool yet, run each creative through this checklist first. If it fails more than two items, hold it back:

Opening hookIn the first three seconds, or at first glance, does the viewer know why this matters to them?Opens on a factory exterior or an animated brand logo

Can predictive scores replace real ad tests?

No, and they shouldn't. A predictive score is a pre-launch reference based on patterns in a large body of past content. What it's good at is ranking: which creatives to test first, which to fix first, which to drop. Actual results still depend on bidding, audience, landing page and market timing.

The right approach pairs scoring with small-budget tests. Scoring saves you the cost of testing obvious losers, and testing tells you how accurate the scores are in your category. After each round, put the actual click-through rate next to the pre-launch score; after a few rounds you'll know which score threshold makes a creative worth prioritizing in your category. The insight
Perceived complexity is the real barrier to creativity.

Cognitive load theory says that when people face too many unfamiliar steps at once, they stall or fall back on old habits. Break creative production into small steps and replace gut-feel guessing with scoring, and the barrier comes down.

Frequently asked questions

Can small sellers use built-in AI ad tools like X Lift and Meta Muse right away?

They're a reasonable starting point. According to Social Media Today, X Lift generates images and copy from a URL and can build an entire campaign, while Meta's Muse takes on marketing tasks by connecting to tools such as Shopify and Canva, and Meta says it won't post or spend money without explicit consent. Start with a small trial, keep approval rights, and judge every tool's output by the same standard.

How accurate are predictive scores? Can they guarantee a creative will perform?

They can't guarantee results. A predictive score is a pre-launch reference based on patterns in a large body of past content, best used for ranking: test high scorers first and fix low scorers. Real performance still shows up only in ad data, because bidding, audience and landing page all affect results. Pair scores with small-budget tests so scoring cuts the cost of testing obvious losers.

Without designers or a native-English team, how do I make sure creatives feel local?

Start by rewriting your selling points the way US shoppers talk: use cases instead of specs, and "what problem this solves for you" instead of "what we have." AI can produce native English copy, but before launch check at least three things: Chinese-style phrasing, exaggerated or noncompliant claims, and whether the scenes and people look like everyday American life.

How often should I refresh ad creatives?

There's no fixed schedule; let the data decide. When a creative's click-through rate keeps falling, its frequency climbs and its cost per conversion rises, that's usually fatigue. Keep a few backup creatives from every test round so you can swap one in the moment a winner starts to fade, instead of starting from scratch after results collapse.
