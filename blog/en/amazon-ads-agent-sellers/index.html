Amazon's AI ad agent: should sellers who can't run ads use it?

"What AI tool can help me run my ads well?" It's one of the questions Amazon sellers ask most, especially those without a dedicated ads person. The short answer: it's worth trying, but don't hand everything over at once. Give day-to-day execution to AI, keep your goals, profit floor and budget caps in your own hands, and let a small controlled test decide whether to trust it.

The question has become urgent because Amazon just put AI at the center of ad buying. According to Chinese cross-border e-commerce news site Cifnews, Amazon Ads unveiled Amazon Ads Agent at its unBoxed 2026 conference: a unified ad platform built around agentic AI that brings together sponsored search ads, display/video/audio ads and full-funnel campaigns, and gives ad recommendations in plain language.

What exactly is Amazon Ads Agent?

Based on the Cifnews report, think of it in three layers:

  • One unified platform: Sponsored search ads, full-funnel campaigns, and display, video and audio ads are all created and managed in one place. You can build ads manually, let Amazon's AI optimize them, or combine the two.
  • Full-funnel campaigns: You supply the budget, products and creative; AI plans, runs and keeps optimizing, putting search, display, video and streaming TV into a single campaign. These are now open to eligible advertisers in Amazon's US store.
  • A conversational assistant: Describe your target audience in plain language, ask questions to diagnose performance, get bid and budget recommendations, and apply them in one click. This part is in closed beta for eligible advertisers worldwide, and availability varies by region.

In addition, DVA+, which combines display and streaming TV ads, launches in the US store from mid-to-late October and will then expand gradually to Amazon stores worldwide.

Amazon's own numbers: advertisers who applied its natural-language recommendations grew unique customers by more than 25% on average and cut cost per impression by more than 10%. These are the platform's own statistics. They suggest the direction is worth a try, but they don't mean your category and margin structure will see the same results.

Why do sellers both want and fear AI ad buying?

Behavioral economics has a concept called loss aversion: people feel a loss far more strongly than a gain of the same size. In advertising, a seller's fear that "AI wasted $500 of my money" easily outweighs the hope that "AI might earn me an extra $500."

Past experience piles on. Plenty of sellers have been burned by outsourced account-management agencies or "guaranteed results" services: the money got spent, the reports looked great, and orders didn't budge. So when a new AI tool arrives, many swing to one of two extremes. Either they never touch it and miss out on the platform's early upside, or they hand everything over at once and write it off entirely the moment something goes wrong.

Healthy skepticism is actually a good thing. It forces you to design a way to check results before you start. Real growth often comes from exactly this "doubt first, then verify" process.

What should you hand to the AI agent, and what must you keep?

The principle: repeatable, reversible execution goes to AI; decisions that affect profit and brand stay with you. How do you write plain-language instructions so the AI stays on track?

An agent that understands plain language still can't guess your business. The vaguer your instructions, the more generic its recommendations. Cover these four elements every time:

  • What you sell: the product, its core selling point, its price range, and its biggest difference from similar products.
  • Who you sell to: who the target customer is and when they buy, for example, "young renters who just moved into a small apartment."
  • What result you want: push volume, win new customers or clear inventory this round. Pick one.
  • What's off-limits: your ACoS ceiling, daily budget, and any keywords or competitor terms you don't want to show up for.

Turn these four into a fixed template and change only the specifics each time. The AI's suggestions will fit your situation better, and you'll find it easier to judge whether a plan makes sense instead of being led along by a professional-looking report.

How do you test it in month one? A four-week controlled test

  1. Week 0: Write down your floor. Work out the break-even ACoS for each key product (roughly your gross margin before ad costs), and set a total budget cap for the test period.
  2. Week 1: Pick test products. Choose one or two products with stable reviews and plenty of stock, and run a small slice of budget on them using AI recommendations or a full-funnel campaign. Leave everything else as is, as your control group.
  3. Weeks 2 to 3: Watch, don't meddle. The algorithm needs a learning period, and frequent manual changes will skew the test. Check the data once a week and stop only if a floor is breached.
  4. Week 4: Compare. Put the test group and control group side by side on new customers, ACoS, cost per impression and total orders.
  5. Decide the next step. If the test group is clearly better, expand gradually. If they're even, give it another round. If it's clearly worse, pull the budget and write down why.

A hypothetical example: a home-storage seller with a $2,000 monthly ad budget sets aside $400 to test AI on two key products, and agrees to pause if ACoS stays above 30% for a full week. Whatever happens, the worst case is known before the test begins, and the anxiety that loss aversion brings shrinks a lot.

Once you're using AI, which numbers should you still watch?

AI will make your reports look better and better, but a few lines tied directly to profit are yours to guard:

  • New-to-brand customer share: Is AI bringing in genuinely new customers, or people who would have bought anyway?
  • Store-wide profit: Ad sales went up, but did they eat into your organic orders?
  • Inventory turnover: Can your stock keep up with the demand full-funnel ads create?

Frequently asked questions

Can Chinese sellers use Amazon Ads Agent now?

According to Cifnews, full-funnel campaigns are currently open to eligible advertisers in the US store; DVA+ launches in the US store from mid-to-late October and then expands worldwide; and the conversational assistant is in closed beta for eligible advertisers globally, with availability varying by region. Your own ad console is the final word on what you qualify for.

Will AI ad recommendations make me spend more?

They could, so set a total monthly budget, a daily cap and an ACoS ceiling in advance. Amazon says advertisers who applied its recommendations cut cost per impression by more than 10% on average, but that's the platform's own statistic. The safest approach is to test with a small slice of budget and a control group, and judge by your own data.

With Amazon's AI, do I still need an ads specialist or agency?

Repetitive bid changes, keyword picks and reporting will increasingly go to AI, but goal-setting, profit accounting, new-product strategy and cross-platform budget allocation still need human judgment. Rather than paying for workload, ask both service providers and tools to prove themselves with verifiable results, such as new-customer counts and take-home profit.

How often should I check the data during the test?

Once a week. The algorithm has a learning period, and changing settings by hand every day disrupts it and skews the results. Step in right away only when spend or ACoS hits the floor you wrote down beforehand; otherwise, log results weekly and compare everything in week four.
