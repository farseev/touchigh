Global Ecommerce Strategy: Why Small Cross-Border Sellers Should Do Less

Selling overseas has moved from getting out there to staying there. For small sellers on tight budgets, focusing on a few parts of the business that reinforce each other often pays off faster than being on every channel.

ToucHigh Research TeamPublished October 16, 20265 min readEnglish
Also available in简体中文
“
The question behind this article

What are the key success factors for running global ecommerce operations, and with limited resources, how should a small seller decide what to focus on?

Short answerThe key success factors in global ecommerce come down to five: one primary market, a few hero SKUs that keep inventory healthy, two or three channels that send traffic to each other, unified data, and brand content that US shoppers and AI can understand. For small sellers on tight budgets, the most effective strategy is often subtraction: concentrate money and people on a few things, prove them out, then replicate.

Key takeaways

  • The focus of selling overseas is shifting from growth to operations; the hard problem is no longer how to get out, but how to stay.
  • Under the law of diminishing marginal returns, every extra channel or SKU adds less new return, while the cost of managing it doesn't shrink.
  • Doing less doesn't mean putting all your eggs in one basket; what matters is whether the pieces you keep send traffic to and reinforce each other.
  • Agentic AI commerce is arriving faster than past shifts, so small sellers should first make their existing product information and content readable by AI rather than opening another channel.

Success in global ecommerce operations doesn't come from how many country sites you launch or how many channels you're on. It comes from concentrating a limited budget, inventory and team on a few parts of the business that amplify each other: one primary market, a small number of hero SKUs (stock-keeping units, meaning individual products), two or three connected channels, and one unified data and content workflow. For small sellers, doing less often pays off faster than doing more.

That shift is also showing up in the industry consensus this year. According to 36Kr (republished by Tencent News), attendees at its 2026 Going Global conference broadly agreed that the focus for companies selling overseas is shifting from growth to operations; the hard problem is no longer "how do we get out there" but "how do we stay." Vivey Wan, Shopify's head of commerce for Asia, noted at the event that the move from ecommerce to mobile commerce took about 15 years, and from mobile to social commerce about 9 years, while the move from social commerce to agentic AI commerce may take only about 7 years.

Meanwhile, an analysis by Ebrun, republished by Huxiu, points out that since the start of 2026 at least 18 cross-border ecommerce companies have gone public or are racing to list, yet "revenue up, profit flat" is common. Even at Anker Innovations, inventory turnover stretched from 70.8 days in 2023 to 87.7 days in 2025. Longer turnover means more cash tied up in stock. If even top brands face this, small sellers with limited capital have even less room to buy growth by listing more and more products.

Have the key success factors for global operations changed in 2026?

The factors themselves haven't changed; their order has. It used to be a race to see who got out first and expanded fastest. Now it's about who can stay and who actually makes money. Drawing on the conference discussion, the key factors fall into five groups:

  • Market: pick one primary market and go deep on local demand instead of casting a wide net.
  • Products and inventory: a small number of hero SKUs, with healthy turnover and cash flow.
  • Channels: two or three channels that send traffic to each other, not ten accounts each fighting on its own.
  • Data: a speaker from Dun & Bradstreet warned at the conference that sales, finance and after-sales data kept in separate places easily leads to bad calls, and a Shopify speaker advised unifying data sources first, then scaling AI applications.
  • Brand and content: in Vivey Wan's framing, brands need to be discovered, understood, trusted and bought in AI channels.

Why do more channels often mean worse results?

Economics has a name for this: the law of diminishing marginal returns. With everything else held equal, if you keep adding input to the same activity, each additional unit brings less extra return. In cross-border operations, your first channel and first hero SKU deliver the highest return. Each channel or product you add after that brings smaller new returns, while the learning curve, content localization and team bandwidth cost just as much as before.

Imagine a seller with a $5,000 monthly marketing budget running six channels at once, averaging under $1,000 each. No channel reaches the volume needed for steady testing and learning, creatives can't be iterated in time, there isn't enough data to draw conclusions, and the seller ends up wrongly deciding that no channel works.

In the chaos of too many choices, less is more. Put the same money into two or three channels and each one generates enough data for good tactics to be spotted and scaled. The same rule holds for SKUs, markets and tools: every one you add means more to manage and coordinate.

Doesn't doing less mean putting all my eggs in one basket?

It's the most common worry, and it deserves a clear answer. Ebrun's analysis also flags concentration risk: one company gets 85.55% of its revenue from Amazon, and another got 93% of its first-half revenue from a single market, Indonesia. Some companies are actively rebalancing; Intellirocks (智岩科技), for example, cut Amazon's share of its revenue by 10 percentage points between 2023 and 2025.

So "less is more" doesn't mean betting on just one thing. What matters is whether the pieces you keep reinforce each other: social content drives branded searches, branded searches and clear product information help AI answers cite you, and AI citations bring people back to your store. Fewer pieces, tightly connected, actually make you more resilient.

How does a "do more" operation differ from a "do less" one?

DimensionDoing moreDoing less
MarketsLaunch several country sites at onceProve out one primary market, then replicate
SKUsLong-tail mass listing, scattered inventoryA few hero products, turnover days under control
ChannelsAn account on every platform, each run separatelyOne sales marketplace + one owned channel + one or two content channels
BudgetSplit evenly, so no channel gets enoughConcentrated so every channel can learn
Tools and teamMore and more tools, siloed dataOne workflow, with data in one sheet
MetricsTotal sales and impressionsGross margin, cash flow and each channel's marginal return

How can small sellers cut back their global operations?

  1. Pick just one primary market. Take the US as an example: keep other markets as they are with no new investment, and focus the team on one market's customers, language and compliance.
  2. Cut SKUs. Rank every product by gross margin and turnover days, drop long-tail items that sit in inventory for months, and save your push for the top few. Fewer SKUs make stocking and logistics easier to plan: hero products can be stocked in warehouses closer to customers for faster delivery, and long-tail items don't have to take up overseas warehouse space.
  3. Cut channels. Keep one main sales marketplace, one owned channel (your independent brand site) and one or two content channels. Pause the other accounts or keep them on basic maintenance.
  4. Concentrate the budget. Make sure every channel you keep gets enough budget for steady testing instead of splitting money evenly, and test new channels with only a small slice.
  5. Unify your data. Put products, inventory, orders and content performance into one sheet or one system, and review it every week.
  6. Review marginal returns every quarter. Compare how much gross margin each extra dollar brings in each channel, and move the lowest-return budget to the highest-return channel.

AI channels are coming fast. What should small sellers do now?

If Vivey Wan is right, agentic AI commerce will arrive faster than any channel shift before it. Agentic AI commerce means AI assistants help shoppers discover products, compare them and even place orders. For small sellers, that doesn't mean opening yet another channel. It means making the content you already have readable by AI.

The lowest-effort approach is to run search and social through one workflow. Write product specs, use cases and FAQs clearly and consistently so they serve both Google search and GEO (Generative Engine Optimization, which gets your brand cited in AI answers from ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews and others), then rewrite the same material into US social content. Frequently asked questions

Should a small seller run Amazon, TikTok Shop and an independent site all at once?

Not necessarily. A steadier mix is one sales marketplace, one owned channel and one or two content channels. Get them sending traffic to each other and working well before you add new channels. The test is whether each channel can get enough budget and attention to test and learn, not whether you're able to open it.

How do I tell when a channel has hit diminishing returns?

Look at the increment, not the total. After putting more budget into a channel, check whether new orders, gross margin or branded searches grow in proportion. If spend goes up while new returns clearly shrink and acquisition costs keep rising, the channel is nearing saturation, and you can move the extra budget to higher-return parts of the business.

Will cutting long-tail SKUs hurt sales?

You may lose some long-tail orders in the short term, but you usually gain healthier inventory and cash flow. Ebrun's analysis shows that even for top brands, longer inventory turnover brings write-down pressure. When small sellers concentrate resources on hero products, their ads, content and reviews all build up faster.

What does agentic AI commerce mean for small sellers?

It means more and more buying decisions will pass through an AI assistant's filter first. At the 36Kr conference, Shopify noted that brands need to be discovered, understood, trusted and bought in AI channels. For small sellers, step one is writing product specs, use cases, FAQs and reviews clearly and consistently so AI can understand and cite them.
