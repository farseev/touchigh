After the Trade Show: Turn Canton Fair Exposure Into Lasting Search Assets

You paid for the booth, travel and samples, but the buzz lasts only weeks. Here's how exporting factories can turn one trade show into a year of search and social content that keeps inquiries coming.

The question behind this article

We spend a lot every year on the Canton Fair and overseas trade shows, and inquiries dry up as soon as the show ends. How do we turn that exposure into a lasting source of leads?

Short answerTreat each trade show as your annual "content-capture day," not just a business-card hunt. Before the show, announce it and book meetings. During it, record buyers' real questions, film process explainers and appear on camera with customers and partners. Follow up within 72 hours, then spend 12 months turning the footage into website articles, FAQs and LinkedIn and YouTube content, so buyers still find you when they search or ask AI.

You spend heavily on the Canton Fair and overseas trade shows every year, and inquiries dry up the moment the show ends. How do you turn that exposure into a lasting source of leads? The answer: treat each show as your annual "content-capture day." Business cards go stale, but the questions buyers asked at your booth, the process demos you gave on the spot and the conversations you recorded with customers and partners can all become content that Google and AI keep finding for a whole year.

The pace of going global is speeding up. According to 36Kr's coverage of its 2026 global expansion conference, Vivey Wan, head of commerce for Asia at Shopify, said the move from e-commerce to mobile commerce took about 15 years and from mobile commerce to social commerce about 9 years, while the shift from social commerce to AI agent-driven commerce may take only about 7. 36Kr vice president Xie Zuoqiang summed up today's challenge as "whether a brand can stand firm overseas, and whether the numbers add up right now." For factories that win orders at trade shows, put those two together: channels are changing, and every marketing dollar has to show a long-term return.

Why do inquiries dry up once the show buzz fades?

The problem usually isn't the show itself. It's that nothing is left once it's over. Business cards sit in a sales rep's drawer, booth photos get one post on WeChat Moments (China's main social feed), and your page in the exhibitor directory looks like thousands of others.

Meanwhile, buyers do one thing once they're back in the US: they look up every one of the dozens of suppliers they met. They search your company name on Google, or ask AI "Is this factory reliable?" If all they find is a website that hasn't been updated in years, the good impression you made at the booth soon gets buried under someone else's.

What do trade shows have to do with SEO, AEO and GEO?

One line on each term:

  • SEO (search engine optimization): making your pages rank near the top when buyers search Google for your category or company name.
  • AEO (answer engine optimization): writing content as questions and answers so Google's featured snippets and AI Overviews answer in your words.
  • GEO (generative engine optimization): getting AI to mention and cite you when buyers ask ChatGPT, Perplexity or Gemini "Which Chinese factories make this?"

Trade shows happen to be the best source of material for all three. The questions buyers ask at your booth, like "What's your MOQ (minimum order quantity)?", "Can you get UL certification?" and "How many days for a sample?", are exactly what they'll type into search boxes and AI tools once they get home. Turn those questions and your answers into web pages, and the value of the show stretches from three to five days to a full year.

How do you run the cost-benefit math on a trade show?

The economic idea of cost-benefit analysis is simple: put all the costs and all the benefits of something side by side. The trouble with trade shows is that almost all the cost lands in a few days, while the benefit, if you only count business cards collected on site, fades very fast.

Imagine an outdoor furniture factory exhibiting at an overseas show and spending RMB 300,000 in total on the booth, build-out, travel and sample shipping. If you only count inquiries from the two months after the show, that money is hard to earn back. But if you bring one extra person whose only job is filming and taking notes, the added cost is small, and the footage can support dozens of articles and videos over the year, so the cost per piece of content drops sharply.

Video is usually expensive because you have to assemble a venue, products, people and questions. At a trade show, all four are already there. In 36Kr's coverage, Sun Kai, CEO of Zhangyue Technology, said the average production cost of AI short dramas fell 75% in three months, yet hits and profits concentrated even more on high-quality content; a separate panel agreed that AI lowers production costs while topic judgment and market insight remain scarce. A trade show gives you exactly the scarce part: real questions from real buyers.

Spending on marketing isn't the mistake; spending and keeping nothing is. An Ebrun analysis republished by Huxiu found that at least 18 cross-border e-commerce companies have gone public or begun preparing to list since the start of 2026, and nine of them have selling expenses above 20% of revenue. The article says bluntly that some companies "rely too heavily on marketing rather than product innovation." The same piece notes that Govee (Zhiyan Technology) is expanding its independent site and offline channels, and says online reputation has supported its offline growth. Online and offline feed each other, and trade shows are no exception.

Collaboration adds another layer of return. When customers, distributors and partners appear on camera with you, they're happy to share the content too, and your name shows up on other people's pages and accounts. To buyers and to AI, what others say about you is often more persuasive than what you say about yourself.

After the show, how do you turn the footage into a year of content?

  1. Sort the footage the night the show ends. Make four folders (process demos, buyer questions, customer interviews, product details) and write a one-line note for each clip while memories are fresh.
  2. Rank the buyer questions. Count which ones came up most; the top ten are your topics for the next three months.
  3. Follow up within 72 hours. Don't just write "Great to meet you." Include a link to the page that answers the buyer's question.
  4. Turn each frequent question into an FAQ or article. Answer it in the first paragraph, then add detail and evidence, so Google and AI can lift it.
  5. Split one video into several versions. The full cut goes on YouTube, highlights go on LinkedIn and TikTok, and the transcript goes on your website.
  6. Let partners speak for you. Publish distributor interviews and customer testimonials with written consent, and invite them to share on their own channels.
  7. Leave a hook for the next show. Keep updating the show landing page; by your next exhibition, it will already be a page with content and rankings.

How do you know whether show content paid off?

Don't just count business cards. Check four numbers every month: impressions and clicks for your company name and category terms in Google Search Console; answers to the "How did you find us?" field on your inquiry form; whether you're mentioned when you ask ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google's AI Mode the same set of questions; and the share of all inquiries that came from content.

Frequently asked questions

The organizer already has an exhibitor directory and online pages. Do we still need our own content?

Yes. Exhibitor directories use a standard format with limited space, so thousands of exhibitors look much the same, and the pages are rarely updated. Only your own website and social accounts can explain your processes, certifications and differences, and build up over time under your own domain and accounts. Use the directory as an entry point, but it can't replace your content assets.

What should we watch out for when filming customers and partners at a show?

Get consent first, ideally in writing, stating which platforms the content will appear on. Keep their unreleased products, prices and order details out of frame. Agree on interview questions in advance and focus on industry issues and working together, not a hard sell. Ask them to approve before publishing and invite them to share afterward; that's how collaboration lasts.

We don't have a professional video team. How do we make trade-show content?

A phone, a lavalier mic and a handheld stabilizer are basically enough. The value of trade-show content lies in real questions and explanations, not polished visuals. The key is dividing roles in advance: assign one person to film and log buyer questions full time, rather than having sales reps film while they greet visitors.

How long should we keep publishing content from one show?

Plan for a full year. In the two weeks after the show, publish the recap and the most frequent questions; after that, pick one clip a week from your library to turn into new content, and write one in-depth article a month. By your next show, the landing page and related content will have built rankings and engagement, ready for the next round of pre-show promotion.
