TikTok Shop US Videos: How Small Creators Plus More Videos Drive Sales

TikTok Shop US sales come from steady, high-volume posting and lots of small creators, not one polished video. Here's a 15-second product test, English hook templates and a creator sampling plan.

ToucHigh Research TeamPublished October 16, 20265 min readEnglish
Also available in简体中文

The question behind this article

I want to grow on TikTok Shop US. How do I make short videos that stop American shoppers scrolling and get them to buy?

Short answerGrowth on TikTok Shop US comes mainly from posting videos consistently and in volume, plus lots of small creators, not one polished hero video. According to Marketplace Pulse data, shops that posted more than 1,000 videos are 8% of sellers but drive 60% of sales. The playbook: pick products you can demo in 15 seconds, batch-shoot two to three videos a day from hook templates, send samples widely to small creators and reward those who keep posting.

How do you make TikTok videos that stop US shoppers mid-scroll? Short answer: on TikTok Shop US, sales usually don't hinge on one beautifully shot video. They hinge on whether you can post consistently and in volume, and get enough small creators posting alongside you. Start with products whose results you can show in 15 seconds, then use templates to turn video into something you can mass-produce.

Two sets of data out this month make the case clearly. The "2026 TikTok Shop US Seller Development Report," republished by the Chinese cross-border e-commerce outlet Cifnews, draws on Marketplace Pulse statistics covering 151,747 US sellers (a September 7–13 snapshot). Shops that posted more than 1,000 videos made up just 8% of sellers but drove 60% of sales; shops with 50 videos or fewer made up 39% and took only 5%. Digiday, meanwhile, cited Flywheel's analysis of 1.5 million creator samples: creators who posted just one video drove $7.19 in sales on average, while creators who posted 20 or more averaged $74.07 per video.

What does the data say: why does posting more beat shooting better?

Read the two data sets together and they point the same way:

  • Sales are highly concentrated at the top. The report shows the top 748 sellers account for half of all estimated cumulative sales.
  • The video gap is huge. Shops with no videos have median lifetime sales of 96 units; shops with more than 1,000 videos have a median of 15,590 units, roughly 160 times as many.
  • You don't need a big account to start. Among the top 1% of sellers by sales, about one in five accounts has fewer than 10,000 followers.
  • One video isn't enough. In Flywheel's data, the more a creator posts, the higher the average output per video. More volume doesn't dilute it.

A caveat: the report's authors themselves note these are correlations and can't prove on their own that video volume directly drives sales. The data also covers only sellers' own accounts, not affiliate creators' videos. Still, the direction is clear enough. The report recommends running TikTok Shop like a publishing channel that never stops updating: winning shops post 1,000+ videos a year, about two to three a day.

Why do so many sellers know they should post more but still can't get videos made?

What holds many sellers back usually isn't budget. It's a feeling that US videos have to look professional: you need a fluent English-speaking presenter, a video editor and a feel for local memes. That imagined barrier keeps people stuck at "we'll start once we're ready."

Cognitive load theory explains the stall. When your mind pictures a task as dozens of complicated steps, it decides the task is more than it can handle, so you procrastinate. What really blocks creativity is often imagined complexity, not ability.

The data punctures that picture: the shops that sell well rely on volume and consistency, not cinematic production. Break "make one great video" into "shoot three 15-second videos a day from a template," and each step becomes small enough that it takes no courage. Output rises on its own.

What kind of video makes US users stop scrolling?

The report offers a simple way to choose products: run a 15-second test. As the report puts it, products whose results can be demonstrated in a 15-second video dominate TikTok Shop. It also shows a median list price of about $23, with 84% of products under $50. In other words, products priced in impulse-buy territory, with results you can see at a glance, do best.

How do you build a pipeline that ships two to three videos a day?

  1. Pick 1–3 hero products. Only choose products that pass the 15-second test; shelve anything whose results you can't demonstrate.
  2. Turn your selling points into 20 hooks. Mine negative reviews, competitor reviews and customer questions, and tie each hook to a specific scenario or pain point.
  3. Set a fixed shoot day. Shoot for half a day each week and film several videos per setup. A phone plus a fill light is enough, and one session gives you a week of footage.
  4. Edit from templates. Standardize caption style, opening length and the closing call to action, so editing goes from creating to filling in a template.
  5. Bring in small creators at the same time. Send samples in bulk using the approach below, so your own account and creator accounts both turn out videos.
  6. Review once a week. Rank videos by completion rate, click-through rate and sales; copy the structure of the top 20% and drop the approaches in the bottom half.
  7. Get the budget order right. The report recommends funding production before ads: build up enough videos first, then use paid promotion to amplify the few that take off.

How do you keep creator samples from vanishing into a void?

Flywheel's data is sobering: only 47% of creators who receive a sample post a video, and only 14% drive a sale. Sending samples isn't a strategy in itself; getting creators to keep posting is.

  • Cast wide, then narrow. Flywheel Ventures CEO Deren Baker suggests sending thousands of samples to build a creator pool, then gradually shifting to long-term, repeatable partnerships.
  • Favor small creators. The coverage suggests small creators may drive more sales per thousand followers. Sending samples to small creators and paying commission per order makes it easier to get multiple videos.
  • Commission as the base, bonuses for the best. Start with commission only, and give your top-performing creators an extra flat fee or performance bonus.
  • Give creators a cheat sheet. Include three hooks to choose from and the key points of a 15-second product demo, so filming is easier for them.

Baker also noted that some brands may need to keep 5,000 videos live every month, a scale he said intimidates many brands. Small sellers don't have to get there in one step, but be clear: volume is the underlying logic of this business.

Frequently asked questions

Can a new shop with no followers still grow on TikTok Shop US?

Yes. The report shows that about one in five of the top 1% of sellers by sales have fewer than 10,000 followers, so follower count isn't a hard barrier. Video volume and consistency matter more, though the report notes this is only a correlation. New shops should post daily on their own account while sending samples to small creators in bulk, building video volume on both tracks.

How many videos a day should I post on TikTok Shop US?

The report observed that winning shops post more than 1,000 videos a year, about two to three a day. Small sellers can start with one a day and add more once their topic, shooting and editing templates are working. More important than the daily count is never going quiet, and dropping weak approaches after each weekly review.

What if sending samples to creators brings very low returns?

Flywheel's data shows only 47% of creators post a video after receiving a sample and 14% drive a sale, so low returns are normal. Widen your sampling first, then focus resources on small creators who will post more, motivate ongoing output with base commission plus bonuses for top performers, and give creators ready-made hooks and demo points.

Do higher-priced products work on TikTok Shop US?

It's harder. The report shows a median list price of about $23, with 84% of products under $50, and products priced over $100 account for only 3% of units sold. For pricier products, use TikTok to build interest and demonstrate the product, then send buyers to your independent direct-to-consumer (DTC) site or another channel to purchase, backed by more reviews, comparisons and after-sales guarantees.
